# CHAPTER 20 — TIE

**Kenji · 5 February 2025 · Shibuya**

Rain. Tuesday. 11:40 p.m.

My son waits outside the station with a bag. He is nineteen. He wears a coat his mother bought. He has my hands, which he does not know.

He opens the back door. He does not get in.

"Papa," he says.

This is the word he uses when he wants money.

I say, "Get in."

"No. I come with you."

"I am working."

"I know." He gets in. He puts the bag on the seat. "I have something."

I look in the mirror. He is looking at the back of my head, not at my face. This is how he sees me now. A back of a head. A man in a front seat.

I wait.

He takes out a tie. Blue. Cheap. A tie from a station shop with the paper still on it.

"For you," he says. "For the interview."

"What interview."

"Not yours. Mine." He looks out the window. "Next week. At a company. I need to look like a person who has a father who wears a tie."

I say nothing.

"I bought one for me too." He holds up another tie. Grey. "We can match. Not the same. Match."

I start the car. Meter: 0 yen.

---

He sits in the back for forty minutes. No passengers. I drive toward Shibuya and then toward Nakano and then back, because he has not told me where to go and I cannot ask, because asking is a thing I do not do.

He talks. Nineteen years old. He talks about the company, about a woman in his class, about the train being late, about the food at university being bad, about a game. I listen in the mirror. He watches the back of my head.

Then he says it. Not loudly. Not as a joke. As a thing said into a window.

"妈, you coming home for dinner today?"

I do not correct him.

I have never corrected him.

The meter moves. 480 yen. 720 yen. The wipers go. The rain is the same rain as before and as 2011 and as every night for eleven years.

He says it again, softer, maybe to the window, maybe to me. "You coming?"

"Tuesday," I say.

This is not an answer. He knows it is not an answer. He puts his forehead against the glass.

"I made rice," he says. "Your — she made too much. She asked me to ask you."

"She asked."

"Yes."

"She knows where I am."

"Yes." A pause. "Everyone knows where you are."

I drive. 960 yen. 1,040. He has the blue tie in his hand and he is turning it over and over, feeling the fabric, the way a man checks steel.

---

He gets out at 12:30. At the door he turns.

"You'll wear it?" he says.

"Yes."

"I'll be at the station at eight. If you come, you come."

"Yes."

He walks into the building. He does not look back. This is a kindness. He has learned it from someone, and I do not know who, and I am afraid to ask, because asking is a thing I do not do.

I put the blue tie on the passenger seat. I do not put it on.

I have never worn a tie in this car.

---

My rating is 4.8. One review. *Driver kept crying.* I read it once a month. It is the only thing in the world that knows me, and it is written by a stranger, in English.

At 1:40 a.m. I park on Maruyamacho, far from the laundromat. The music comes through the wall. Quiet. The voice says the sentence.

*"You are allowed."*

I listen to it the way a man listens to a meter.

A woman in a grey sweatshirt comes out of the laundromat once in a while. She does not see me. Or she sees me and decides I am a taxi, which is safer.

I have a tie on the passenger seat. A son who called me mother. A wife who asked him to ask me. A voice in a wall. I have heard it for four months and never named it.

I know the shape of the voice. I do not ask who put it there.

I still do not say this out loud. This is not the same as not knowing.

---

2:58. The music stops under the street. The lights come on somewhere I cannot see.

I put the tie in the glove compartment, with the 2011 evacuation map and the unused wet wipes. It does not fit. I close it on the tie and the map and the wipes and the small space where a man could keep a thing if he wanted to.

Meter: 1,240 yen.

He said *mother* twice. I did not correct him either time.

Eight p.m. Tuesday. Station. I will not go.

I have never gone.
