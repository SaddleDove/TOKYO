# CHAPTER 23 — FORM

**Theo · 28 February 2025 · Immigration office, Shinagawa**

The immigration office in Shinagawa is beige in the way only a building designed by a committee can be beige — twelve plastic chairs, nine occupied, a woman with a printer that sounds like a small animal in distress, a man asleep standing up, which I did not know was possible until this country decided to teach me, and a child with a red backpack who looked at my sunglasses and then looked away, which is the correct response to a man in sunglasses indoors at nine in the morning.

I took ticket B-17. The screen said B-03. This is a country that gives you a number before it gives you a chance, which is very fair really, because if they gave me a chance first I would only use it to say something funny and then we would all be in trouble.

I fill in the form.

Surname: A-d-e-y-e-m-i.
Given names: Theo.
Date of birth: 14/06/2000.
Nationality: British.
Passport number: copied twice. Wrong the first time. Correct the second.
Address: 4-8-2 Maruyamacho, Shibuya-ku. The room with the leak. I write it without the leak.
Employment: none.
Sponsor: none.
Purpose of extension: performance.

I stop at *performance*. I write it again. The pen is a cheap pen from a convenience store and it presses too hard.

Purpose of extension: performance.

The screen says B-09.

A woman at desk two calls a name. Not mine. Another. A man gets up and goes and comes back ten minutes later with a stamp on a paper and a face like a man who has been let out of something.

B-12. B-13. B-14.

The child with the red backpack drops a crust of bread. The mother does not see. I pick it up and put it on the bin. The child watches me do this. I do not smile. This is not a thing I decided. It is a thing my face did.

B-16.

B-17.

The officer calls my name. She says: *Tee-oh Add-ee-em*.

I say, "Yes."

She says: *Tee-oh Add-ee-yem*.

I say, "Yes."

She looks at the form. She says: *Tee-oh A-day-me*.

I say, "Yes."

This is three. After three you stop correcting, which is a rule I have learned in nine months and which is the same rule as *don't explain the joke twice*, except that in this room the joke is your passport and nobody is laughing. After three, you take the name they give you and you carry it out of the room and you do not say it to anyone on the train, because on the train someone might ask you to spell it, and then you would have to do the whole thing again with a different vowel, and I have a show on Saturday and a very limited supply of vowels.

She checks the passport against the form. She checks the form against the passport. She uses a ruler, or something like a ruler, a small clear plastic thing with a line on it. She puts the line under my date of birth.

"Working," she says.

"No."

"Working no."

"No."

"Money."

I show her the bank app. Four hundred and eleven thousand yen. The money is from Mei, who pays me for shows in cash in an envelope with no words on it, and from two Tuesdays at a bar in Shimokitazawa that I did not tell her about, and from a man who tipped me four thousand yen in a basement in Shibuya and told me nothing.

She looks at the screen. She looks at me. She looks at the sunglasses, which I have not taken off, which is a mistake I know I am making and which I am making anyway.

"Why glasses."

"Medical."

"Medical."

"Yes."

She writes something. I cannot see what. The form has a box that is empty and she does not fill it and does not tick it, and the empty box is the loudest thing in the room, which is impressive, considering the printer is still going and that small animal is really putting its back into it. An empty box is a very British thing, by the way. It's the national gift. We give them to people at airports and then we say *nothing to declare* and we mean everything.

"Address," she says. "This address is share."

"No. One room."

"One room." She writes. "Landlord know you live."

"Yes."

"Contract."

I have no contract. I have a text message from a man called Sato with a photograph of a leak and the words *your fault* in Japanese, which I have translated four times and which I now know means something else entirely, which is *the rain is your fault*, which is a joke, I think, or a statement of weather.

I put the phone on the desk. She looks at the photograph. She looks at the leak. She looks at my face in the screen reflection.

"This is not contract."

"No."

She writes. The pen makes a small sound. The woman with the printer prints something. The child with the red backpack sits on the floor and plays with the crust of bread.

She says a sentence in Japanese. I know six words. I know *form*, *Tuesday*, *please*, *more*, *wait*, *no*. She says *more* and *Tuesday* and a word I do not know, and then she says, in English, carefully, "Twenty-eight February. Bring paper from landlord. Bring paper from —" she stops and looks at the sunglasses again — "from doctor."

"Doctor."

"Yes. For glasses."

"It's not —"

"Twenty-eight February."

She puts the form in a tray. The tray has six forms in it. Mine is the seventh. She calls the next number. B-18 does not come. A man with a grey coat stands up from the row behind me and goes to the desk and sits down where I was sitting.

I take my passport. I take the receipt with the date on it. I put the sunglasses back on, which I had taken off for a moment and do not remember taking off.

Outside. Steps. A man smoking by the door. A woman with a pram. The train signs are in four languages and I read all four and none of them say what I am doing here.

At 3:07 I am outside WASH & GO. The password tonight is about a letter. I say it wrong. Momo looks at me. Behind him a woman's voice says, "Let him in. Any letter. Let him in."

Inside. Bass. Pink light. Mei behind the bar. Rin behind the speakers. Yusuf by the wall. The old man in row three moves his glass a finger's width to the left.

Mei puts a glass in front of me.

"Ginger," she says.

I drink. The receipt is in my pocket. It has a date on it like the vials have a date on it, which is to say: a real date, not a feeling, not a maybe.

At 00:17 the voice comes on. *You are allowed.*

I take the receipt out and look at it under the pink light. February twenty-eight. Fifteen days from the show. Five weeks from the end of the building as the notice still says.

I put it back in my pocket and go to the scaffolding, because there is a show to do and the cardboard officer is retired and someone has to be funny in a room that has a date in it.

They did not stamp the paper. They gave me a date instead, which is the immigration service's way of saying *we'll get back to you*, which is the British way of saying *we've already forgotten you*, except that here they write it down, which is rude but efficient, and I respect efficiency in a country that has made a fine art of making me wait.
