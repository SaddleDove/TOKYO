# CHAPTER 33 — ORIGINS

**Theo · 15 March 2025 · TOKYO**

Fire code says forty-eight. Tonight there are forty-eight.

Mei counted at the door like a bouncer at a function, which she is not, which is a job I have now seen her do twice in four months and which she is frighteningly good at. Momo stood beside her with his hand out and his face like a held breath, and when the forty-ninth person arrived she said, in English, "Not tonight. Tonight is a number."

The person said, "But last week —"

"Last week was seventy-one. Tonight is a number. Come Tuesday."

This is the first time I have seen a room choose to be smaller, and I want to write about it in a way that would make a foundation cry with joy, but I am not filming, and I have decided not to turn anything into a sentence tonight, including myself.

---

Momo gave me a paper bag before I went up. No note. Inside: my sunglasses. Black. The patch case empty, cleaned out, folded neatly. I held them for a bit in the mirror room under six mirrors, and I put them on, and I took them off, and I put them on again, because a man can change his mind about a face four times in thirty seconds and call it a creative process.

There was a piece of masking tape stuck to the mirror. STAY. I did not write it. I know whose hand it is, which is a thing I would not have known a month ago, and which I have decided not to say out loud, because the room has heard enough voices in English for one lifetime.

At 00:10 I went to the scaffolding. The wig was on the stool: a black bob with a fringe that could cut rice paper. Mei had ironed it. She will never admit this. I found a small crease in the fringe that was ironed flat and I know an ironed crease when I see one, because my mother ironed everything in our house in E13, including, once, a plastic bag, which she then wore out to the shops, which is a different story.

I said, "You ironed the wig."

"No."

"You did."

"Drink first."

"Mei."

"Break a leg. Drink first."

---

I wrote ORIGINS in four parts. I want to be exact, because nobody filmed it and exactness is the only record it has.

**Part one: breathing.**

I came out as Theo. No wig. No sunglasses, in the end, because I took them off at the last second and put them on the stool beside the wig. Black shirt. Face washed. The patch on my hip under my trousers where nobody could see it and where I had put it carefully, which is a thing I have done four hundred times and which I will do again, and which is not the show and which is part of the show, which is the whole problem with bodies.

I played a voice note. Three seconds. My mother's breathing. In a room of forty-eight people in a basement in Shibuya at midnight, the smallest sound in the world came out of a cheap speaker and nobody moved, and I let it run its whole three seconds, which I have never done at home, where I always skip to the end because hearing a person breathe at me for three seconds is too much to survive in a kitchen alone.

Then I stopped it. I said, "That's my mother. That's the whole message. She sent it for two years and one day she sent two words and I haven't replied."

A laugh. A small one. Not at me. With me. The room knows the shape of a mother not replying.

**Part two: names.**

I said, "In this club we don't use real names. This is a rule. I broke it on my first night and a woman behind the bar slid me a drink and told me to drink first, which is the most useful sentence I have learned in this country."

Mei, from the bar: "Still true."

I said, "So I'm not going to use yours. I'm going to use the ones you gave yourselves, which are the only ones that count, which is a thing I believe, which is a thing I said loudly for twenty-four years in a city that didn't care, and which I am saying quietly here, where you all care too much."

I named no one. I looked at the old man in row three. I looked at Yusuf by the wall with his hands out of his pockets. I looked at Rin behind the speakers with one hand on the cassette player. I looked at Cass at a table with her camera in her bag and her hands in her lap. I looked at the cat on the bar.

"VEX," I said. "That's mine. It's not a name I was given. It's a name I made. Some of you made yours in a room like this. Some of you are still making it in your head and haven't said it yet, which is fine, which is also a name."

Then the bass came in very low, and I did a lipsync in four languages, none of which I speak, to a song about a woman who leaves and a man who cannot follow, and the room sang the chorus in the words each of them knew, and for three minutes we were all wrong in the same key, which is the closest thing to a country I have ever felt.

**Part three: the form.**

No cardboard officer. No joke. I promised.

I took a piece of paper out of my pocket. A real form. The one from Shinagawa, folded into eight, with a box on it that the officer did not fill and did not tick, and the empty box was the loudest thing in that room, and I wanted forty-eight people to see it.

I held it up. I walked the length of the small stage with it. I put it on the floor and stood on it. Then I got down, picked it up, and folded it again, and put it back in my pocket, which is what a person does with a form when there is no other thing to do with it.

Nobody laughed. This is the truest thing I did all night.

**Part four: origins.**

I took off the black shirt. Under it I had a dress. Not a costume. Black. The same black as the one in the bag under Yusuf's bed in Shibuya, with cement on the left side. I put it on over my head in front of forty-eight people under pink light, and I did not make a face, and I did not wink, and I did not say *isn't this funny*, which is the sentence I have used for six years to stop anyone from looking too closely.

Then I put the wig back on. VEX arrived. VEX has cheekbones you could cut rice paper on. VEX does not wonder if her mother listened. VEX does not have a visa. VEX is not from anywhere and is therefore allowed to be from everywhere, which is a very English way of saying a very simple thing, and which I have spent six years getting wrong.

I did the last song without the joke at the end.

This is the thing Mei asked for. No joke at the end. I got to the last line, and the old muscle in my mouth reached for something about landlords or forms or my mother's stew, and I shut it, and I finished the song on the actual last note, and then I stood there with my hands down under the fluorescent light, which came on at three exactly, as it always does, as it must.

Forty-eight people. No photographs. No phones. No livestream. The old man in row three put four thousand yen on the table and did not leave. His hands were shaking, which I have never seen before, and he kept them flat on the table, the way a man holds a thing steady when it is not his to hold.

---

At 3:06 I was still on the stage. At 3:12 I sat down on the edge of it with the wig in my hand and the dress on my body and the sunglasses on the stool beside me, and I looked at the room under the lights, which is a room that has no secret any more and is therefore just a room, which is the best thing a room can be.

Rin came and stood next to the stage. She put two fingers on the sunglasses on the stool and moved them a finger's width to the left, which is a thing she does with the pill counter, twice, once to do the thing and once to make sure.

"Are they yours?" she said.

"Yes."

"Do you want them?"

"Yes."

"Then take them."

I picked them up. I put them in my pocket. I did not put them on, because a person can want a thing and keep it in a pocket, and this is a new skill and I am very bad at it.

"The show," she said.

"Yes?"

"It was a door."

There it is. Her word. I have waited four months for her to use it about something I did, and I can't think of a single funny thing to say, and if I say *thank you* it will become a scene, and Mei will throw a broken opener at me, and she has excellent aim.

So I said, "Drink first."

She almost smiled. I saw it. Six mirrors would not have shown it better.

---

At 3:20 the room was empty except for us and Mei and the cat. The last Saturday. One show. No cameras. Forty-eight people who will never be in a film and who will therefore be the only ones I remember exactly.

Mei put a glass on the bar in front of me. Ginger. Everything here is ginger. I am becoming a person who associates ginger with being allowed.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow."

"No joke," she said.

"No joke."

She poured. She did not look at me, which is how I knew she had heard every word of the form part, and that it was okay, and that it was not okay, and that both of those things were in the room and neither of them needed a photograph.

Outside, the rain waited, the way it waits. In four days there will be a wall where the dryer was, and a club that is not anywhere, and a man in Berlin who will hear the lights come on at three in a room with no alley and will make one, which is something I know now that I did not know when I got here, and which I am not going to write down, because a thing you write down becomes a poster.

Four hours. Then the lights.

I got the last one right. I did not add a joke at the end.

I am counting that.
