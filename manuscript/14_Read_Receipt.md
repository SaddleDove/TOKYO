# CHAPTER 14 — READ RECEIPT

**Theo · 12 January 2025 · Shibuya**

New Year in this country is a thing that happens twice. Once on the thirty-first, with a train and a temple and a bell, and once on the first, when everyone goes back to work and pretends the first part was a holiday and not a nervous breakdown with good lighting.

I spent the thirty-first in 梅屋 with three customers and a television showing baseball. Mei said New Year was a children's holiday and adults should not participate. Then she poured me a drink and said, "You are a child. Participate."

At midnight the television showed a countdown in a studio full of people in sequins, and outside the station a million people stood in the cold and counted down to a number, and inside the bar a man fell asleep on the counter with his forehead in a bowl of nuts. It was the most honest New Year I have ever had.

Then my phone lit up.

Not a voice note. A message. From my mother.

This is the sentence she sent:

*I listened.*

That's it. Two words. No *I miss you*. No *why did you leave*. No *are you eating*. Just: *I listened.*

I read it four times and then I turned the phone over on the bar, because a person can only be humiliated by hope a finite number of times per evening, and I had reached my quota.

---

Here is the thing about being cut off by your family. Nobody believes you. You tell a friend and they say *but do they know you're okay?* and you say *yes*, and they say *then what's the problem?* as if the problem were logistics. The problem is not logistics. The problem is that a person can send you three seconds of breathing for two years and you will play it every time, and then one day they send two words and the whole building moves.

I told Rin about it at 3:07 outside the conbini. She was eating a rice ball standing up, because she eats all her meals standing up, like a woman who has never been given a chair and has decided not to ask.

"She listened," I said.

"That's good."

"To three seconds of breathing."

"Maybe she listened to the breathing."

"Rin."

"I am trying."

We stood there under the light that makes the pavement look like a hospital. She had a small cut on her finger, from a tamagoyaki pan, I think, or from a form. She has a lot of small cuts. She never explains them.

"Are you going to reply?" she said.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because if I reply, then it becomes a conversation, and if it becomes a conversation I will say everything, and if I say everything she will have to answer, and I am —" I stopped. "I am not ready for her to answer."

"Then don't."

"Is that honne or tatemae?"

"Yes."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only one I have."

I laughed. Then I didn't. She has this effect. She gives you a joke and takes it back before you can hold it.

---

January in Tokyo is dry and expensive and very clean. The city washes itself in December and spends January being pleased with itself. The trains run. The vending machines glow. Everyone wears a mask except me, and I am the only person in the street with a cold, which I have had since Christmas, which I deserve, because I stood in the rain for eleven minutes on my first night and told a laundromat owner I sold makeup.

Mrs. Saito has started leaving a cup of coffee on the counter for me. She still says my makeup is suitable for office work. I have stopped correcting her. One day she will find out I am a drag queen and she will say, *yes, of course, I knew*, because women like her always knew, and they are never surprised, and they never once say *I told you so*, which is a kind of mercy.

At 1 a.m. on the eleventh I went into WASH & GO and she was there with a basket of towels and she said, "You are sad."

"No," I said. "I am tired. Different."

"Sad is tired with a reason."

I stood there. A dryer was going. The machine that holds the door to the best room in this country was turning with somebody's underwear in it, and I was having a breakdown in a laundromat at one in the morning with a woman who had known for a month and said nothing.

"My mother wrote to me," I said.

"Good."

"Two words."

"Good."

"You don't know what they were."

"Does not matter. Two words from mother is good." She folded a towel. "My daughter sends one word. *OK*. Every Sunday. That is her whole life in one word. I am very lucky."

"She's in Taiwan?"

"She is in Taiwan." Mrs. Saito looked at the dryer. "One word is enough if it comes every Sunday."

I thought about three seconds of breathing. Eleven hours. Four thousand yen from a man in a grey coat. A room that turns its lights on at three and doesn't care who you are when they do.

"Right," I said. "Fine. Two words is enough."

"Then why are you sad?"

"Because I want three."

She put the towel down. Then she did something I have never seen anyone do in this country: she put her hand on my arm, very briefly, and took it away, and went back to folding.

"Three is greedy," she said. "Greedy is good. Greedy means you are still here."

---

At 2:58 I was on the scaffolding in TOKYO doing a number about a man who cannot fill in a form, which is not funny in January and which I did anyway, because I promised Mei no visa joke on Christmas and Christmas is over and I am a woman of my word for exactly eleven days.

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I have started noticing that the sentence is in English. Why would a voice in a basement in Shibuya speak English? Why would a voice from a cassette bought in a flea market say the one thing I needed in a language I could understand? This is a question I keep putting in my mouth and taking out again, like a sweet I am not allowed to eat.

Rin says she doesn't know whose voice it is. Mei says *the room knows*. Cass wants to know everything. Yusuf says nothing and stands near the wall.

I looked at the speakers and then at Rin behind them, and she was watching the cassette player with her face completely empty, like a person listening to a stranger say something about her that she has already decided to believe.

"Rin," I said later, in the alley. "The voice. It says *you are allowed*. In English. To a room full of Japanese people."

"Yes."

"Why English?"

"It was a man with English."

"Who?"

"I told you. I don't know."

"You keep saying that like it's a small thing."

"It is not a small thing." She put her hands in her sweatshirt pockets. "It is a thing I have decided not to pick up."

There it is. I have heard that sentence in this room four times, and every time the person said it they looked at the floor first, which is a thing I have started doing too.

In London I would have picked it up. I would have turned it over, read the label, shouted at it, taken a photograph. This is what I am good at. This is what I came here to unlearn, apparently, because I have spent a month in a room where nobody picks anything up until three in the morning, and the room has made something in me that I don't recognise.

It is quiet.

---

At 3:07 I took out my phone. The message was still there. The two words were still there.

My thumb went to the reply box and stayed there. I could write: *I'm in Tokyo. I'm doing a show. I'm not wrong anymore. Please say something else.*

I did not write it. I put the phone face down on the plastic table and watched the cleaning truck go past.

One day she'll send three words. Or she won't. Either way, two words are sitting in my pocket, and they are mine, and I am not going to spend them tonight.

I walked back to WASH & GO and said good night to Mrs. Saito through the glass, and she waved a towel at me, and I went upstairs to a room with a ceiling leak and a landlord who texts me photographs of it, and I slept for four hours, which is more than some people get.

The lights come on at three. Somewhere under the street, the room does too.
