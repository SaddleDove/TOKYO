# CHAPTER 15 — DOOR MONEY

**Mei · 28 January 2025 · 梅屋 / TOKYO**

The twenty-eighth is payday for a company that does not exist. I like the twenty-eighth. It is a good number. Even. Boring. A number that does not ask questions.

The money moves at 9:03 a.m. while I am at my father's apartment with a razor in my hand. He sits in the bath with his cardigan on and he holds still for me, which is a thing he never did when he was well. His head is small now. Everything is smaller. The man who used to walk through a room like he owned the floor has been reduced to a chair in a bathroom in Nakano with a cardigan floating around him in the water.

"妹妹," he says. "Have you eaten?"

"I ate."

"Don't lie to your mother."

"I'm not lying to my mother."

"She is dead."

I stop the razor. Then I start again.

He did not say that. He said something else and my head put the words in the wrong order, which is a thing heads do in January when they have not slept. He said *sister*, and then he said something about the sea, and then he looked at me with his own eyes, which are still his eyes, and said nothing else for twenty minutes.

I shave what is left. I wash the cardigan in the sink. He does not notice, which is a mercy, and then he notices, which is not a mercy, and he says, "Who took my cardigan," in the voice of a man who has been robbed in his own bathroom.

"I did. It was wet."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't take my cardigan."

I put the cardigan back in the bath. He does not put it on again. He sits there with his hands in his lap and the water cooling around his knees, and I dry his head with a towel, and he says thank you in Japanese, which he never used to say, because Japanese was for customers.

---

The clinic called at noon. The doctor said the word for *progress*, then the word for *unfortunately*, then a number that was not a number, which is the way doctors say *there is no number*. I wrote nothing down. There is nothing to write down. I have a ledger at the bar and I do not put this in it, because the ledger is for people who came in, and my father has never once come in.

He is in a room in Nakano that costs 180,000 a month. My lawyer says the settlement money will last eleven more years if I am careful. My lawyer says *if I am careful* in the voice of a man who has never been careful about anything in his life.

Eleven years. That is a long time and not long enough. I have been in this country sixteen years and I have spent four of them shaving a head, and I will spend eleven more, and somewhere in that arithmetic there is a woman I used to be who owned a failing bar in Changhua and drank too much and did not look after anybody.

She is not dead. She is just busy.

---

At 4 p.m. three customers in 梅屋. One hiding from his wife. One asleep. One who is definitely a tourist because he is photographing the jar of forty-seven broken bottle openers again.

He asks me why I keep them, in Japanese, and I answer in English because his Japanese is worse than mine.

"They have personality."

He takes a photograph of the jar. I let him. A broken thing in a jar is a safe thing to photograph. Nobody gets hurt.

The television has baseball on. In January. The television does not care about months, and neither do I.

At 5:10 Theo comes in with a cold and a face like a man who has been wounded by two words. I do not ask. He does not tell. He sits at the bar and puts his phone down face first, which is a thing people do when a piece of plastic has defeated them.

"Ginger," he says.

"No."

"Please?"

"No."

"You are a cruel woman."

"I am a business." I pour the ginger. "You are a regular. Different product."

He drinks. Then he says, "I got a message from my mother."

"Good."

"Two words."

"Good."

"You don't know what they were."

"Do not tell me." I wipe the counter. "If you tell me, it becomes a thing with a witness. Better to keep it in your pocket."

He looks at me. "You are very good at this."

"At what?"

"At not asking."

I have practiced thirty years. I am almost perfect.

---

At 22:55 the club. Rain. The password tonight is a proverb about a door, which is funny because I am thinking about doors, and I do not tell anybody because a coincidence is not a story.

Seventy people, one cat. Theo does the number with the broom. It is about cleaning. He has stopped calling it a visa joke but it is still a visa joke and the room knows it and laughs anyway, which is a kindness of a particular kind: laughing at a thing because someone needs to laugh, not because it is funny.

Yusuf is by the wall. He has an envelope in his pocket; I have seen him touch it four times. He does not dance. He stands with his hands out of his pockets, which he has started doing since Christmas, and it makes him look like a man who is about to be given something.

Cass is at a table with the camera in front of her and her hands in her lap. She has not filmed for a month. This is either growth or a strike and I do not care which, because the room is quieter without the little red light.

Rin is behind the speakers. She is doing something with the cassette player that involves a screwdriver and a great deal of concentration, and I know she is not fixing it, because it is not broken. She is touching the thing that holds the voice. I have seen people touch things like that. It is not mechanical.

At 00:17 the voice comes on. *You are allowed.*

The room keeps dancing. The room always keeps dancing. This is the whole miracle and nobody notices it because they are busy being saved by a sentence in a language most of them do not speak.

---

At 2:40, between songs, the landlord's son comes in. Not the landlord. The son. He stands at the door in a suit that costs more than my fridge and he does not come in, because he is afraid of this room in the way only a man in a suit can be afraid of a room: with curiosity and contempt in equal measure.

"My father says the twenty-eighth is the last payment," he says.

"He says many things."

"This is different."

"I wiped the counter."

He puts an envelope on the bar. I do not touch it. 財務大臣 jumps up and puts one paw on it, and for one second the cat is a better lawyer than either of us.

"April first," he says. "Eight a.m."

"Eight a.m. on April first. Very thorough."

"The crew needs the space."

"The crew can have the space. It's their space now."

He goes. He does not look at the room, which is strange, because the room is right there and it is the only interesting thing in the building. Men like him never look at rooms. They look at envelopes.

After he goes I take the ledger out and open page 214. There is one line left that I wrote on 28 November: *someone came in.* Under it I write:

*2025 — still here.*

Four words. A small bill. A thing that is true today and may not be true in two months, which is the definition of a promise in this country.

I close the book. I put it back behind the bottles nobody orders. The money from the company that does not exist has already moved into the account, and the club will be open tomorrow night at eleven, and at three the lights will come on, and nobody will have paid for the four hours they took, which is the best business model I have ever had.

Door money. That's what Theo calls it, joking. He is not joking.

I sell doors that open. Even the ones that don't last.
