# CHAPTER 5 — VEX

**Theo · 6 December 2024 · TOKYO**

Right, so — picture this. You spend twenty-two years being told you are too much. Too loud, too tall, too Nigerian, too gay, too *negative space*, apparently, whatever the hell that means, my mother used it once and I still don't know. Then you get on a plane to Tokyo with £400 and a suitcase that smells like your childhood bedroom, and you find out that here, you are not too much. You are simply *wrong*. Different category. Upgraded, technically. Like going from "too spicy" to "not food."

VEX is the only version of me that has ever made sense. VEX has cheekbones you could cut rice paper on. VEX does not wonder if her mother listened to the voicemail. VEX arrives.

The problem with Tokyo — the city, not the club, don't get me started on the club, the club is the only honest room in this country — the problem with the city is that it sold me a poster and I bought a life. Neon. Rain. A man in a coat turning around. I came here for the turning around. Instead I got a 4.5-tatami room with a ceiling leak and a landlord who texts me pictures of the leak to remind me the leak is my fault.

And yet.

Tuesday night, six people, a scaffolding stage, Mei pouring something that tastes like regret and ginger, and this tiny Japanese pharmacist woman who looks at me like I'm a prescription she hasn't decided how to read — and for four hours, I am not wrong. I am not too much. I am exactly the right amount of person for this room.

Four hours. Then the lights come on and I am Theo again, 24, visa in eleven months, mother on read.

Four hours is a lot, though. Four hours is more than some people get in a lifetime.

---

My first proper show was a Tuesday. Not the Tuesday I arrived. A week later, after I had learned three things: the password changes on Sundays; Momo will let you in if you say *rain* with enough feeling; and if you put the patch on the left hip it stays put during lipsync and if you put it on the right it does not, which is a scientific finding I would like published in a journal with a very small readership.

I called the number again. The microphone feedback came and I said, "Good evening, I'm VEX, and I'm going to be your emotional damage for the next forty minutes," which got a laugh from six people and a worried look from the seventh, who turned out to be a man in a suit sitting in row three who had no English but knew the tone.

That's the thing about drag. You think you're performing in English. You're not. You're performing in *tone*. Everyone knows the shape of a woman being dismissed even if they don't know the words.

I did a lipsync to a song my mother used to play while ironing, which is a choice I have never explained to anyone and would like to be never asked about, thanks. Then I did a comedy number about my visa, which involved a cardboard immigration officer and a lot of miming of forms, and then I came back out as myself — Theo, no wig, no glue, just a black shirt and my sunglasses, because the patch had started to peel and I was not doing a striptease for a room full of strangers who had been kind to me.

That's when the old man cried.

Row three. Small. Grey coat. He had been there since opening and he had not touched his drink. When I came out as Theo he put his hand over his mouth, like he had swallowed something and then thought better of it. Then he took his coat off the chair, put four thousand yen on the table — four thousand yen, for a man in a grey coat in a basement — and left before the last song.

I watched him go. I have done this job in three countries and I have never seen a man pay that much to leave.

"Who was that?" I asked Mei.

She didn't look up from the glasses. "A regular."

"Does he come often?"

"Yes."

"Why was he crying?"

"Because you were good."

"No." I shook my head. "That's not it. It was — I said something in English that he can't understand, and he looked at me like I'd read his diary."

Mei put a glass on the shelf. Then she said, in that voice of hers that arrives before the words, "Maybe he knew the English."

"Does he speak English?"

"No." She wiped the counter. "He listens to a room that isn't here."

I have no idea what she meant. I wrote it down anyway. I write down everything people say in this club, because I am collecting a country and I have very little luggage.

---

Afterwards, in the mirror room, I sat with my face in my hands and the wig on the stool beside me looking like a small useless animal. Rin came in and stood at the door.

"You were good," she said.

"Thank you."

"Not good. Something else. I don't have the word."

"In Japanese?"

"In English." She frowned. "I don't have the word in either."

"Try."

She thought about it. I watched her think. She has a way of standing very still, like a person waiting for a train that has already left.

"Like a door," she said. "You were a door."

"Doors are important here," I said. "We sell them."

She didn't laugh. She never laughs at the jokes. She laughs at things that aren't jokes, which is much worse, because then you can't tell if you're funny or if you've accidentally been honest.

There was a strip of masking tape on the wall with a word on it. 本音. I pointed. "What's that?"

"Honne."

"What does it mean?"

"True feelings." She took the tape off the wall and put it back, straight. "We don't translate it."

"Why not?"

"Because then it becomes a small thing." She looked at me. "Your English is too big for some words."

"That's the nicest insult I've ever had."

"It's not an insult."

"I know. That's what makes it worse."

---

Later. 3:07. Conbini. Rice balls under fluorescent light, and Rin standing beside me eating one standing up because neither of us has a table at home that isn't also a bed or a desk or a place to put the pans.

The voicemail from my mother came in at 3:02. Three seconds. I played it twice.

"She breathes at me," I said.

"Maybe she says something."

"No. It's breathing. I can tell the difference. I've been breathing for twenty-four years."

Rin bit into her rice ball. She chewed for a while. Then she said, "My mother sends money. Every month. She calls it insurance."

"So she knows."

"Yes."

"And you?"

She looked at the plastic wrapper in her hand and folded it into a very small square. "I think she is waiting for me to say it."

"Why don't you?"

"Because then it becomes a thing." She said it the way you'd say *then it breaks*.

I wanted to say: *Say it. Just say it. My mother didn't say it for twenty-four years and now we're both here, two children in a car park outside a FamilyMart at three in the morning, and I am the one who flew nine thousand miles to stand in a room that closes at three, and I am still the one who has to tell you to open a door.*

Instead I said, "Fair."

"Fair," she said.

And we stood there. Two rice balls. One small square of plastic. The lights inside the shop were so bright they made the pavement look like a hospital.

That's the thing nobody tells you about Tokyo. It isn't neon. It's fluorescent. Neon is for the posters. Fluorescent is for the truth.

---

On the walk back I stopped outside WASH & GO and looked at dryer four for a bit. Mrs. Saito was inside, folding towels, and she waved at me through the glass and shouted something that I am fairly sure was *your makeup today is very suitable for office*.

"Good night!" I shouted back.

I have been in this country nine days and I have already told a laundromat owner that I sell cosmetics, cried on a train, begged a non-binary bouncer with the word *rain*, and been paid four thousand yen by a man who cried at my face.

I am doing very well, all things considered.

Tomorrow I'm going to ask Mei for a show of my own. Not a Tuesday slot. A whole night. No cameras — I'll insist on that, even though I don't know why yet — no livestream, no phones, just forty-eight people in a room with the lights coming on at three. I'll call it *ORIGINS*.

I have no idea where the name came from. I do. I just don't want to say it out loud yet.

Some things have to stay in the mouth a bit longer before they become true.
