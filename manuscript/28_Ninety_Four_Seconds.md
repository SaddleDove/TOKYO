# CHAPTER 28 — NINETY-FOUR SECONDS

**Cass · 2 March 2025 · Shibuya**

Deletion is not a clean act. This is the first thing I learned. People talk about deleting footage as if it were a moral gesture with a beginning and an end, like signing a cheque or leaving a room. It is not. It is a series of small decisions about which folders to open, and in each folder there is a face, and each face is a reason not to delete the next one.

I started at 4 p.m. in a 4.5-tatami room in Shibuya with a ceiling leak and a landlord who texts photographs of the leak. I had forty-one minutes of footage in one folder and one selfie I could not delete in another. This is what I had instead of a mother, which is a sentence I have been building for twenty-six years and have finally said out loud in an empty room with a plastic cup of water on the floor.

The folder was called ROUGH. Not *subjects*. Not *the people*. ROUGH, which is a word that means *not finished* and also *not careful*, and I have been both.

I opened it.

Rin at the sink. Three minutes and eleven seconds. Wet hair, grey cotton, red knuckles, an object with a label I could not read.

I put my finger on the delete button. Then I took it off. Then I put it on again.

This is the whole moral architecture of my life, repeated in private, with no witness, which is worse than doing it in front of someone, because there is nobody to stop me and nobody to forgive me.

---

I have a theory about why I could not delete it. The theory is this: I did not want to keep *her*. I wanted to keep the version of myself who was capable of taking it, because that version is young and certain and has a grant, and I have been nothing but uncertain for two months, and certainty is a substance people become addicted to in their twenties.

The second theory is worse and shorter: I wanted the footage.

I am aware of the literature. I can cite four scholars. I can cite myself. This is the problem with self-awareness: it is a very comfortable place to live and the footage is still there to be deleted.

At 7 p.m. I deleted Rin's clip.

It took eleven seconds. When it was gone I put my hand on the table and sat there for a while, and the room did not change, and I did not become a better person, which is the thing I had been secretly waiting for, the small click, the moral click, the sound of a lock opening.

There was no click. This is the truth I would put in the grant report if I were honest, which I am not.

Then I deleted everything else.

Yusuf by the wall, hands out of pockets, four minutes and eight seconds. Theo on the scaffolding with a broom, ninety seconds of a joke about a form which was not a joke. Mei behind the bar watching hands, two minutes and forty seconds, in which she does not say a word and looks at six different glasses and nothing else. The old man in row three with his glass moving a finger's width to the left, which I did not know I had filmed and which appeared in the folder like a small accusation.

I deleted the sound of a room I cannot get back. This is the other thing nobody tells you about deletion: it is not the image that hurts, it is the room tone. The bass under everything. The voice coming through the wall saying a sentence in English to a room that does not need translation.

I kept one file.

Ninety-four seconds. I have not shown it to anyone. I will not show it to Rin. I have told myself this is because she does not want to see it, which is true, and because showing it would make it about me again, which is also true, and because I am frightened of what is on it, which is the only true one that matters.

---

I took the SD card out of the camera at 9 p.m. and held it in my hand under the desk lamp. A small grey rectangle. It weighs nothing. It has held forty-one minutes of people who did not agree to be in my film, and now it holds ninety-four seconds, and I have carried a thing around for four months that I kept telling myself was *different* from using it.

It is not different. It was never different. This is the sentence I have been walking toward for four months and I have finally arrived, and there is no room at the end of it, just a desk lamp and a leak in the ceiling and a plastic cup of water on the floor.

I put the card in an envelope. I wrote nothing on the outside, because a name on an envelope is a claim, and I am trying to stop making claims. Then I took a cloth napkin from my bag — from an aeroplane, from the flight in, from nine thousand miles up when I was still calling this project *Tokyo* and still believed a place could be a method — and I wrapped the card in it.

I wrote a note. It took me forty minutes to write one sentence.

*I was looking for my mother. I'm sorry I used your face to do it.*

I put the note in the envelope. I did not sign it. This is not anonymity. This is the smallest possible version of *I am sorry* that I can send without asking for anything back, which is a distinction I have spent twenty-six years failing to understand and which I finally understand now, in a 4.5-tatami room in Shibuya at 9:40 p.m., holding an envelope like a small animal that might bite.

---

At 23:30 I went to the club with the envelope in my pocket.

The password tonight is about a window — the one about a letter. I said it wrong. Momo held out his hand. Behind him a woman's voice said, "Let her in. Any letter. Let her in."

Inside. The room was full. The cat was where he always was. Theo on the scaffolding with no sunglasses on and no joke at the end of the number, which I noticed, and which I did not film. Rin behind the speakers with her hand on the cassette player, pressing a button twice, once to do the thing and once to make sure it had been done.

I stood at the back for a while with my hands in my pockets, which is where they have been for a month, and then I walked to the sink and put the envelope on the stainless steel next to her.

She looked at it. Then at me.

"The card," I said. "It's yours. All of it. What's left."

She did not pick it up.

"I deleted the rest," I said. "I kept ninety-four seconds. I don't know what's on it. I mean — I know what's on it, but I haven't watched it since the night I took it, and I am not going to, and I am not going to tell you what I think is on it because that would be another thing I took from you."

She put her hand on the envelope. She did not open it.

"Why ninety-four?" she said.

"I don't know."

"Try."

I thought about it. The room was loud. The pink light was cutting her face in half and she was standing very still and waiting, which she does, which she is good at, which I have spent four months turning into footage without once understanding that it was a skill and not a texture.

"Because it's the length of a song," I said. "No. Because it's the only part where the room is quiet and you're not in frame. You're off to the left. You're — you're looking at someone, and the camera caught the direction of your eyes and not your face." I stopped. "It's not about you. That's why I kept it. I kept the part where you were looking at something else."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she picked up the envelope and put it in the pocket of the too-big sweatshirt.

"I don't forgive you," she said.

"I know."

"Don't say *I understand*."

"I won't."

"Don't cite anything."

"No."

"Don't tell me about your mother again."

"Okay."

She turned back to the cassette player. Then, after a while, she said, "Ninety-four seconds."

"Yes."

"That's a long time."

"Yes."

"Longer than four hours."

I did not answer. She had taken my one good sentence and turned it around, which is what people do when they have been filmed and have decided to speak, and I deserved it, and I am going to carry it for a long time.

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

The room kept dancing.

I stood at the sink with my hands empty and my camera in my bag, and for the first time in four months I did not reach for either. My hands stayed empty. This is not growth. It is a fact. Facts are the only things I trust now, and I have very few of them.

On the street at 3:14, the cleaning truck going past, the rain starting again in the way rain starts here — not falling so much as being decided — I took out my phone and opened the foundation's email and typed one line in reply.

*I am no longer able to complete this project.*

I sent it before I could read it again.

Then I walked to the station with my hands in my pockets, and I kept them there, and I have kept them there every night since, except when I am holding a cup or a pen or somebody's hand, which is a different thing entirely, and which I am learning.
