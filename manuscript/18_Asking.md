# CHAPTER 18 — ASKING

**Theo · 10 February 2025 · TOKYO**

I am from a family where nobody asks. My mother tells. My father tells. My grandmother told in a language I did not understand and I understood it perfectly, which is the whole problem with tone. *Eat. Go. Don't. Come here. Don't come here.* Twenty-four years of imperatives and I turn twenty-four and move nine thousand miles to a country where the most common sentence is a soft one that means *perhaps, if you insist, no*.

The first time Rin said *don't tell me what to do*, I heard it as a door closing. The second time, thirty seconds later, I heard it as a door closing twice, which is a thing people do when they want you to understand that it is not a draft.

I said, "I wasn't telling you."

The bass came back on. She looked at me with a face that had decided not to hear me, which is a thing faces do, and she went.

I stood in the alley with my hands in my pockets and my jacket gone and my cigarette gone and my joke gone, and 財務大臣 looked at me from the bin with the expression of a small grey man who has audited better men than me.

"Fine," I said to the cat.

The cat did not answer. Good cat. Best cat. A cat who does not answer is a cat who has understood the assignment.

---

The thing is, I *was* telling her. I was telling her to tell her mother. I was telling her to call the city again. I was telling her to take the thing out of the box and put it on the table where I could see it, because I am selfish and because I love a resolution and because I have a visa that expires in eight months and a show in March and a very limited supply of emotional infrastructure.

I told myself I was helping. This is a thing I say to myself often, in the mirror, usually while applying glue, which is a job that requires honesty about what is a face and what is paint.

In London I had a friend called Nigel who used to say, *Theo, you don't listen, you wait for your turn to talk.* I said, *that's what listening is, darling*, and he said, *no, that's what a debate is*. I did not speak to Nigel for a year. Then he moved to Bristol and sent me a message with a photograph of a dog, and I forgave him, because a dog is a piece of evidence that cannot be argued with.

Rin is not a dog. Rin is a woman with eleven phone calls and seven pans and a name in a box, and when she says *don't*, the correct response is not a better argument. It is to shut up, which is a skill I do not have and which I have been practising in a country that is very good at it.

---

At 23:40 I went into the mirror room. She was there, standing in front of a mirror with my jacket folded over the stool beside her, not wearing it, which is a sentence all by itself.

Six mirrors. Glue. Powder. Ginger in the air. The strips of paper on the wall: *You look good today. Don't be afraid. Come back before three.* Someone had added a new one in a hand I did not know. It said, in Japanese, something I could not read, and under it in English, very small: *also come back after*.

I stood at the door. She saw me in the mirror and did not turn around.

"I have a sentence," I said.

"I know."

"I said it last night and the music took it."

"The music takes everything."

"Can I say it again?"

She looked at me in the mirror. Then she turned. Her hair was wet. She had been crying or washing her face, and I did not ask which, because I have learned something in eight weeks.

"I wasn't telling you," I said.

"Yes."

"I was asking."

She said nothing.

"I'm asking you," I said. "I'm not telling you. I'm asking. I want you to tell your mother. Not because it's right. Not because the West says so. Not because I can't bear it. Because I want to know you after, and I can't know you if you keep the door shut and only let the money through it."

This is not what I planned to say. I planned to say something funny and then something true and then leave the room before the true thing landed, which is my usual structure. Instead I said one long thing with no jokes, and now it is in the room, and I cannot pick it up.

She looked at the folded jacket. Then at me.

"Why do you want to know me after?"

"Because before is four hours."

"Four hours is a lot."

"It is not enough."

"It is more than a lot of people get."

"I know." I said it too fast, because that is my defect, and because she had used my own sentence against me, which she does, which I deserve. "I know. I know. I have said that to myself every night since I got here. Four hours is more than some people get in a lifetime. I am very grateful. I am so grateful I could scream. But I am twenty-four and I have eight months on a visa and I want to be a person for more than four hours, and when I want things I say them badly and loudly, and I'm sorry."

She stood there. I have never seen her stand still for so long. In this country stillness is a language and she is fluent and I am a beginner with a phrasebook.

Then she said, "I have seven pans."

"Rin—"

"One for the first roll, which is always wrong. One for the second, which is always correct. One for the days I don't eat. The others are for weather." She picked up the jacket and held it. "You asked. So I am telling you. This is what asking gets."

"I know."

"You don't know. But it's a start."

She put the jacket on, properly this time, and pulled the sleeves down over her hands, and she looked at herself in the mirror, and the mirror held her without asking, which is the thing this room does that I have never understood.

"Will you tell me yours?" she said.

"Mine?"

"Your mother. The two words. You have them in your pocket. You never take them out."

I took my phone out and put it on the table. I did not show her the screen. I said the two words out loud, in the mirror room in a basement in Shibuya, to a woman who was wearing my jacket and looking at me through six reflections.

Then I said, "Now you know."

"Yes."

"Does it make it smaller?"

"No." She turned to the mirror and smoothed the collar of the jacket. "It makes it a thing with a witness. Mei was right. But it's fine."

"Why is it fine?"

"Because the witness is leaving in eight months."

She said it without cruelty. It is a fact, and facts are the only things she trusts. Then she walked past me and out of the mirror room, and I heard her go back to the sink, and the bass came back on, and the room did what the room does.

I stood there with my phone face down on the table and my sunglasses in my hand, and for once I did not put them back on.

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I have heard that sentence fifty times. Tonight it sounded like a question.

---

Later, in the alley, I did not smoke. I gave the cigarette to the cat, which is a joke, and the cat did not take it, which is a better joke.

I told Yusuf about the show in March. I told him there would be no cameras and no phones and that I wanted him to be in it, standing by the wall with his hands out of his pockets, or dancing, if he wanted, in a dress or not in a dress, it did not matter, because the room did not care what you wore as long as you came back before three.

He said, "I cannot dance."

I said, "I know."

He said, "You are always telling me I can."

"No," I said. "I'm asking."

He thought about this. He is a man who thinks slowly and in English that is almost correct, and he has a way of tilting his head like a man listening to a beam settle.

"Okay," he said. "I will ask Aldi."

"Good."

"He will say yes. Then I will be afraid. Then I will do it."

"That's the whole thing," I said. "That's ORIGINS."

The cat came out from behind the bin and sat between us. Yusuf said *otsukaresama* to it, which is wrong, and the cat accepted it, which is a kind of grammar I will never learn.

At 3:03 the cleaning truck went past. The street was bright and empty and expensive, and my jacket was on a woman walking home in the rain, and my two words were in the air in a mirror room somewhere under the street, and I had asked for something instead of telling, and it had not killed me.

This is the first honest thing I have done in this country that nobody filmed.

I am counting those.
