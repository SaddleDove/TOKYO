# CHAPTER 9 — CONCRETE

**Yusuf · 27 December 2024 · Shibuya construction site**

The building is forty-seven floors. I am on thirty-one and I put the steel where the steel goes. I know where it goes. I have put it there nine hundred times.

Down I do not look. Up I look and I am afraid. Iqbal says this is backwards. Iqbal is not afraid of anything, even his wife, who is a small person with a loud voice and who once came to the site and shouting at him in front of forty men, and after she left he said, *she is small but she has the volume of a country*, and we all laughed, including the foreman, who understood none of the words.

Friday. 6:40 a.m. Cold. The kind of cold that gets into the steel and stays there.

The foreman says something about the wind and we go up. The wind is not bad. The wind is a man touching you when you did not say yes, but today he is gentle. This is what I think at 6:40.

We are putting the cross-brace on the east side. Iqbal is on the other end of the beam. He holds his end the way he holds everything, which is fully, with his whole body. He is singing. He sings a song from a film, a love song, in English he does not know, and I know it because my sister played it at her wedding in Bekasi and I know all the words in my head but not in my mouth.

"My heart will go on," he sings, badly, beautifully.

"Your heart is in Makassar," I say.

"My heart is everywhere. This is my problem."

He laughs. Then the beam moves.

This is the thing about steel. You put it where the steel goes or the building falls. But a beam is a thing with two ends, and if one end is not where the other end thinks it is, then the beam has an opinion, and the opinion is physics, and physics does not care about your wife or your singing or the fact that you are thirty-one floors up.

I do not hear a sound. This is the thing I tell people later and they do not believe me. There was no sound. There was a space where Iqbal had been, and the beam where the beam goes, and the wind still gentle, and my hands still on the steel.

Later there are boots. Iqbal's boots, size forty-two, by the door of the site office. Nobody has moved them. It is four days before someone takes them, and when they do, I do not ask where they go.

---

The police come. A man in a suit comes. The foreman cries, which I have never seen a foreman do, and it makes me angry for a reason I cannot say in Japanese or Indonesian or English.

They ask me questions. I have two hundred Japanese words and I use forty of them. I say *I saw*. I say *he was there*. I say *then not there*. The man in the suit writes things down, and I think he is writing my words but he is writing a form, and the form has boxes, and the boxes are more important than Iqbal.

At 19:00 I go to the club. I do not decide to go. My feet go. This is a thing about bodies: they are smarter than passports.

The password tonight is about a door. I say it wrong. Momo looks at me. He must know, because he looks at my hands, which have cement on them, and at my face, which has the face of a man who has just seen a person become a space. He holds out his hand. He does not say anything. He never says anything. He is the best person in this building.

Inside: the room, the music, the pink light cutting faces in half. Rin is behind the speakers. Theo is on the stage with a microphone that is not working yet. Cass is in the corner with her camera down.

I go to the mirror room and stand in front of the mirrors for twenty minutes. The mirrors do not ask. This is why I come here.

In my wallet I have a photograph of my mother. She is standing outside our house in Bekasi with a bucket. I take it out and put it on the shelf next to the old hairpin.

"Otsukaresama," I say to the photograph. Thank you for working. Or: you are tired. I do not know which. My Japanese is two hundred words and all of them are almost correct.

Then I cry. It is not a big cry. It is a small mechanical thing, like the signal finishing on a call. Forty minutes, then the screen goes black. This is shorter than that.

---

Theo comes in while I am wiping my face with my sleeve. He does not say *are you okay*. He says, "Darling, your sleeve is wet and that is a fabric decision I respect," and he puts a glass in my hand, and then he stands next to me at the mirror.

"My friend died," I say.

"I know."

"How you know?"

"Your hands." He looks at them. "They are shaking and they don't usually shake."

We stand there. Outside, someone is doing a lipsync and the room is laughing, and the laughing and the standing are in the same building and this is not strange to anyone except me.

"You want to dance?" he says.

"No."

"Good. Me neither." Then, after a bit: "Someday. Not tonight. But someday, I'm going to put a dress on you and you're going to dance in it and the whole room is going to be quiet because you'll be the only real thing happening."

"You are crazy."

"Yes. This is my brand."

He goes back to the stage. I put the photograph back in my wallet. I say *otsukaresama* again, to the mirror, to the shelf, to the empty place where a man was at 6:40 this morning, and I get the grammar wrong again, and I say it anyway.

At 02:59 the lights come on. Every face gets old. Mine gets oldest.

I go home. In the morning I will go back to floor thirty-one and put the steel where the steel goes, because a building does not stop wanting to stand just because a man stopped singing.
