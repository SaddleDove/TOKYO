# CHAPTER 25 — CELL

**Rin · 15 February 2025 · Nakano → Shibuya**

Mei spent the night in a cell.

I found out at 6:40 from a man called Chen who works at 梅屋 on Mondays and who has a face like a folded receipt. He called me because he did not know who else to call, and because Mei had written my number on a piece of paper taped to the register under the word *if*.

"She is okay," he said. "She is at the station. They kept her one night."

"One night."

"Yes. One night. She has cough."

I put on the same sweatshirt and went to the station. It is a thing I do without deciding. The pharmacy is closed on Saturday until nine and I have the key and I did not take the key, which is a detail I notice later, in the train, when I am already halfway to Nakano.

---

Mei's father was on the floor.

Not dead. On the floor. This is a thing I have learned to distinguish in the last year: the difference between a body that is still here and a body that has gone somewhere else. He was still here. He was in his bathrobe with one shoe on and the other shoe under the table, and he had fallen getting out of the bath, and there was a small cut above his eyebrow that was not bleeding much but was the right shape to worry about.

He was looking at me when I came in.

"妹妹," he said. "Have you eaten?"

"I ate."

"No," he said. "You don't eat. She doesn't eat."

Who she was, I do not know. Me, probably. Or his wife. Or his sister. In this family the word for *you* moves around the room like a person looking for a seat.

I called an ambulance. I called the clinic. I called Chen back and told him Mei's father had fallen and Chen said, in a very small voice, "I cannot go to the hospital. I have the bar."

The bar. The bar is open at three and closed at six and Chen is sixty-one years old and has a mortgage, and there is no one else.

"I'll go," I said.

"You work at nine."

"I'll go."

I sat with the old man on the floor for eleven minutes until the ambulance came. He held my wrist — the left one, the inside, where the scar is — and he held it with a strength that a man who cannot stand still has, and he said nothing else, and I did not pull away, which is a thing I would have done a year ago.

---

At the hospital a doctor used the word for *observation* and the word for *unfortunately* and a number that was not a number, which is the way doctors say *there is no number*. I have heard this sentence in three rooms in the last six months. It is always the same sentence.

Mei came at 11:20. She had a cough and a paper with a number on it and a face that had decided nothing that has been opened from the outside and does not approve.

"Don't look at me like that," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are looking with the whole face."

"I'm looking with one eye. The other one is for the vial box."

"Good." She sat down. "Your eye is very busy today."

We sat in the hospital corridor. The chairs were hard. There was a television showing a drama in which a woman was crying in a hospital corridor, which is a thing television does when it wants you to be comforted by a mirror.

"One night," she said.

"Chen told me."

"One night in a room with a woman who cried. Not me. Her. She cried for two hours. I told her to stop. Then she stopped and asked me why I was in a cell, and I said, *because I own a room that closes at three*, and she said, *that's a nice room*, and then she slept." Mei coughed. "The cough is from the crying woman. Or the cell. Different."

"Your father fell."

"Yes."

"He is okay."

"Yes." She looked at the door with his name on it. "He was trying to get out of the bath to make rice. For me. He has not made rice in nine years." She put the paper down. "He wanted to make rice."

I did not say anything. There is a thing you say in this situation in English and in Japanese and in Taiwanese, and it is the wrong thing in all three, and I know it, and I keep my mouth shut, which I am very good at.

At 12:10 she said, "He called me sister today."

"Yesterday."

"Today. Every day now." She stood up. "Come on. I need to go to a bar that is failing and open it, because a man is hiding from his wife and he has nowhere else to go."

---

At 23:00 the club was open. Mei behind the bar with the cough and the paper in her pocket. Seventy-one people, one cat. The cat came out from under the speakers and sat on the bar and looked at Mei and then at me and then at Mei again, and Mei said, "Don't. You already had your night."

Theo was on the scaffolding doing a number about a form. He has stopped calling it a joke, and the room has stopped laughing at it in the way you laugh at a joke, and started laughing at it in the way you laugh at a wound you recognise. Yusuf was by the wall with his hands out. Cass was at a table with the camera in her bag, which is where it has been for two months.

At 00:17 I pressed play. The voice came on. *You are allowed.*

The room kept dancing.

I looked at the door behind the bar that goes to the alley and I thought about a taxi parked on Maruyamacho that I have seen twice and never once approached, and I thought about a tie in a glove compartment, and I thought about a man who had a voice before I had a name, and I put my hand on the player and I did not pick anything up.

At 2:11 I went outside for air. The alley. Three bins. Broken light. The cat followed me, which he does now, which is new.

A taxi was parked at the end of the street. Not close. Far enough that the sign was small.

I stood under the broken light and looked at it for a while. The taxi did not move. Nobody got out. The window was down a finger's width, which I could not see from where I was, but which I have decided was down a finger's width, because that is the kind of detail I invent when I have decided not to ask a question.

"Otsukaresama," I said, quietly, to the taxi.

The word came out correctly. That was the strange part. I stood in an alley and said a word in my own language and it went nowhere, and the taxi did not answer, which is what taxis do.

The taxi did not answer. At three the lights came on inside, and through the wall you could hear the room change, and I went back in, because the room needed me at the switch, and because there is a difference between standing outside a door and going in, and I have spent my whole life standing outside doors.

Not tonight. Tonight I am a woman with seven pans and a vial box and a mother in Kobe who is waiting for one word, and I have already said it to an alley, which does not count, and to a taxi, which cannot hear, and to a room that has heard a voice in a language most of it does not speak for four months.

One person. That's all I want. One.

I am standing next to the person. I do not know yet.
