# CHAPTER 35 — EQUINOX

**Rin · 20 March 2025 · Shibuya**

Spring equinox. Day and night are the same length, which is a fact the city does not care about, and which I have been thinking about since four this morning.

At eight a.m. the crew came. Not seven. Not nine. Eight.

I stood across the street under the awning of a closed flower shop with a coffee in my hand that I had not paid for, because Mrs. Saito had put it in my hand and said, "Today is free," which is a thing she has never said in twenty-two years and which I have decided not to ask about.

Two men in hard hats went into WASH & GO first. They took out the dryers. Dryer four came out at 8:41, which I know because I looked at my phone, and because counting is what I do. They carried it to a truck and put it on top of a pile of other machines, and it looked small up there, which was strange, because it was a door.

Then they took the wall.

Not all of it. They took a section behind where the dryer had been, which is the place where the stairs went down, and they worked with a saw that made a sound like a person shouting in a language I could not understand, and concrete dust came out and went into the street and got on the shoes of a woman waiting for the bus, who brushed it off without looking, because in this city people brush things off without looking, which is a skill I have spent four months learning and will now never need.

At 9:20 a man came out of the building carrying a piece of the sign. The light box from the old pachinko hall, with half of a character missing. He put it in the truck. I wanted to take it. I did not take it. A thing you take becomes a thing you have to explain, and I have spent my whole life not explaining, and I am tired, and I am still not taking it.

At 10:02 the stairs were gone. There was a flat wall where they had been, the colour of the rest of the room, and a man was sweeping the dust into a pile.

This is the whole demolition. Two hours and forty-one minutes. I had imagined it would take longer, or be louder, or that someone would come out, which nobody did, because there was nobody in there, because the room had emptied itself at three the night before and had not taken anything with it.

---

Mei was at 梅屋 at 3 p.m. with three customers, one hiding from his wife, one asleep, one new, who asked about the jar of forty-seven broken openers and whether they were for sale.

"No," Mei said.

"I'll give you five thousand for one."

"Which one?"

"The blue one."

"That one has the best personality. Ten thousand."

The man laughed. Mei did not. She was wearing a black dress under her apron, which I had never seen her wear, and which she did not explain, and which she will never admit to, the way Theo will never admit about the wig.

At 5:30 the last customer went. She turned the television off, which was showing baseball in March, and she took the ledger out of her bag and put it on the bar in front of me.

"This isn't an inheritance," she said. "It's a bill."

I opened it. Two hundred and fourteen pages. I have read four of them, and I will read the rest, and I will not read them tonight, because tonight is for leaving, and reading is for staying.

"I can't take this," I said.

"You can't not take it." She put a glass down. "Drink first."

"Mei."

"Drink first. Then argue."

I drank. Ginger. Everything here is ginger. 

"You should call your mother," she said.

"Do not tell me what to do."

"I am asking." She wiped the counter, which was clean. "I ask because I am leaving tomorrow and I will not be here to ask again. My father is in a hospital in Nakano. He called me sister yesterday and by my name the day before and neither of those is me, but I shave his head anyway, because that is a thing I can do, and it is a thing he lets me do, and that is the whole marriage, and the whole disease, and the whole daughter."

I put my hand on the ledger. The cover was red and the corners were soft from eleven years of hands.

"Why me?" I said.

"Because you came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one."

"That's in the book."

"Yes. I wrote it. You should not read your own line. It's embarrassing."

"Are you happy?"

"No." She poured another glass. "Both. I am alive, which is not a small thing to write. Ask me again in a year."

---

At 6 p.m. I went home. My room has six tatami and a wall for a window and a shelf of seven pans and a box of vials in the fridge with my name on them now, which is a sentence I have said to no one and which I will say to one person someday, and I do not know who, and I have stopped caring that I do not know who.

On the table: the recorder, the cassette, the screwdriver, and a blue tie that I took from a man who held it out. I have not worn it. I have not put it in a drawer either, which is a thing I would have done a year ago.

There is a paper bag on the table. It was not there this morning. Theo must have left it before dawn, because he cannot give a thing directly and keep his face straight. Inside is a strip of masking tape with STAY written in my own hand and a pair of black sunglasses, cleaned of patches and folded neatly, with no note.

I put them on. They are too dark for a room with a wall for a window. I took them off. I put them on again. I did the thing four times in thirty seconds and called it a creative process, which is a sentence I learned from Theo, who learned it from nobody, who made it up in a laundromat at 3:06 a.m., which is the kind of thing a person makes up in a laundromat at 3:06 a.m.

Outside, the sky was the colour of a washed glass. The light was the same length as the dark, which is a fact and not a feeling, and which I am going to hold onto, because facts are the only things I trust now, and I have very few of them.

I called my mother.

It was 7 p.m. in Shibuya and 6 p.m. in Kobe. She answered on the second ring.

"Did you eat?" she said.

"I ate."

"Don't lie to your mother."

"I'm not lying to my mother."

A pause. A long one. The kind of pause that has four years in it and fifty thousand yen a month and a word neither of us has ever said in the right order.

"Rin," she said.

"Yes."

I sat down on the floor with the phone against my ear and the ledger on the table in front of me and the sunglasses in my hand and the vial box in the fridge with my name on it, and I said the word I have had in my mouth for four years, which is not a big word, which is a small word, which is the smallest word that is still big enough.

I said: "I am your daughter."

She did not answer right away. Then she said, "Okay."

One word. Not a poem. Not a forgiveness. A word.

Okay.

It was enough. It was not enough. Both, which is the only honest answer I have ever given anyone, and which I gave to a woman who asked me if I was happy, and which I gave to a taxi driver who said *you were nine*, and which I gave to a room that is now a wall.

At 8 p.m. the lights came on somewhere in this city. There is no club. There is no alley. There is no door behind a dryer. But a room with the lights on is a room with the lights on, and I am standing in one, and there is nobody here to tell me to leave, and I am not going to.

The rain waited.

It always waits.
