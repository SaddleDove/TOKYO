# CHAPTER 11 — ORIGINS

**Theo · 24 December 2024 · TOKYO**

I have a theory about London, which is that everyone in London is from somewhere else and nobody has told the city yet. My mother is from Lagos. My father is from a part of London that his father was not from. I am from E13, which is a postcode and therefore a country with no flag and very bad weather.

When people ask me where I am *really* from, I say London, and they say *no, where are you really from*, and I say *E13*, and they say *no, but where are your parents from*, and by the fourth round everyone is tired and someone has said something about curry. This is a game with no winning move. I have played it at every job I have ever had, including the one where I was a drag queen in a pub in Peckham and a man said, genuinely, *but where do you get the confidence from?*

I said, *From being asked that question every week for twenty-four years, darling.*

So: ORIGINS.

The name came to me in a laundromat at 3:06 a.m. while I was pretending not to sleep standing up. There was a dryer going and a woman folding towels and a word on the wall in marker that I could not read, and I thought: *origins*. Not where I am from. What I have made of it. The word arrived whole, like a wig in a box, and I have been carrying it around ever since, and if anyone asks me where it came from I will say *the laundromat*, which is true, and leave out the part about the voice in the wall, which is also true and much less funny.

---

I asked Mei at 5 p.m. in 梅屋, which smells like someone's grandfather's kitchen and baseball. Three customers. One hiding from his wife. One asleep. One who is definitely a tourist because he is taking a photograph of the broken openers, which is the most Tokyo thing I have seen and I have been here a month.

"One night," I said. "A whole night. Not a Tuesday slot. ORIGINS. Forty-five minutes of drag, then —"

"No."

"Let me finish."

"No. You have not said the important part yet."

"I have — "

"You have not." She wiped the counter. "Say the important part."

I took a breath. This is a thing I have learned to do in this country. Breathe first. Talk second. Revolutionary.

"No cameras," I said. "No phones. No livestream. No Cass."

There. I had said it. It came out wrong — I heard it come out wrong — because *no Cass* is a person, and you are not allowed to say a person like that in a sentence about a show.

Mei put the rag down. She looked at me for a while, in the way bartenders look at hands.

"You want a room with no door," she said.

"A room with a door that opens."

"Same thing. Different word."

"Is it?"

"Yes." She picked up the rag again. "People can come in. They cannot take it out. This is the whole trick. You want to be seen, not captured. I understand this."

"Mei —"

"Do not thank me. I hate it."

"I wasn't."

"You were in your face. It was there."

I shut my mouth. I have a face that says things before I do. It is one of my defects, along with the sunglasses indoors and the voicemail habit and the fact that I fall in love with rooms.

"One condition," she said.

"Anything."

"Do not say anything tonight."

"I don't understand."

"Tomorrow night. Christmas. You usually do a number at 1 a.m. about the visa, with the cardboard officer. Don't."

"Why?"

"Because a room that closes at three is a room with a deadline. You know this. I know this. Everybody else comes here to forget it. You keep reminding them." She poured something into a glass and pushed it at me. "Give them one night where nobody counts."

I drank. It was ginger. Everything here is ginger. I am becoming a ginger person. My mother would be furious. She makes a stew with ginger that could strip paint and she has never once apologised for it.

"No cardboard officer," I said. "Fine."

"Good."

"No visa joke."

"None."

"No mention of the landlord."

"Absolutely not."

"No —"

"THEO."

"Yes. Sorry. I'll do a song."

"Do a song." She turned to the television, which was showing baseball in December, because the television in this bar has opinions about nothing and seasons less. "And no sunglasses on stage. You look like a man hiding from an optician."

"They're not fashion," I said. "It's medical."

"Everything is medical. Still take them off."

---

Cass found out in the club at 23:50. I told her myself, because I am a coward but I am not a coward in the way that lets other people find things out from bartenders.

"No cameras," I said.

She had the camera on her knee. She looked at it, then at me, then at the camera again, like a person checking that the thing she loves is still where she left it.

"You're asking me not to document the most important thing that has happened to me in this country," she said.

"I'm asking you to be in it."

"I am in it. That's the problem. I'm in it taking notes."

"Then put the notes down for forty-five minutes."

She laughed. It was not a happy laugh. It was the laugh of a person who has read four scholars and agrees with all of them.

"You know what that is?" she said. "That's a privilege. You get to say *don't film me* because you've already been seen. You've been seen your whole life. Being looked at is your native language. I'm asking to look because looking is the only thing I've ever been good at."

"That's not —"

"And I know it's extractive. I know. I wrote a paper about it. I can cite myself."

"I'm not asking you to not look," I said. "I'm asking you to look with your eyes."

That stopped her. It came out before I could put a joke on it, which is how the important sentences always come out of me, badly dressed and early.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she said, "My mother left when I was four months old. I have a photograph of a woman holding a baby in a hospital in Oakland, and I have spent twenty-six years making films about rooms I was not in, looking for a person who is not in them."

"I didn't know."

"You didn't ask."

"I didn't."

"Fine." She put the camera on the table and did not pick it up again. "Forty-five minutes. But I get to be in the room with my eyes."

"Yes."

"And if you use the word *authentic* I will leave."

"Deal."

---

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I stood at the back of the room with my sunglasses in my hand because Mei had told me to take them off and because Rin was standing near the sink looking at me, and I wanted her to see my face doing something that wasn't a joke.

The room was full. Seventy-one people, one cat, forty-eight on the permit. Yusuf was by the wall with his hands in his pockets. The old man in the grey coat — I had looked for him, and he was there, row three, with his drink untouched — was watching the speakers, not me.

"We need a name for this," I said, to nobody in particular.

Mei, from the bar: "You have one."

"I have four. Theo. VEX. Sir, when the airport man is angry. *Darling*, when I'm lying."

"You want the fifth."

"Yes."

She poured. She did not look at me. "Then earn it."

So that's the plan. Forty-five minutes. No cameras. No cardboard officer. One room, forty-eight people on paper and seventy-one in fact, and a name I have to earn like a thing at an airport, which is to say: with forms, with waiting, with a person behind a desk deciding whether I am a problem.

I have eleven months on the visa. The room has ninety days. Neither of us is good at arithmetic when we're happy, which is why I have decided not to do it tonight.

Tomorrow is Christmas. Tomorrow the lights come on at three and nobody leaves. I don't know why yet. I just know that I will be standing there when it happens, with no sunglasses on and no joke in my mouth, and I will be frightened, and I will stay.
