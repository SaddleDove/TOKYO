# CHAPTER 10 — TAXI METER

**Kenji · 21 December 2024 · Shibuya**

Rain. Saturday. 1:40 a.m.

Passenger, male, foreign, drunk. Smells of gin. Gets in the back. Says Shibuya. I say Shibuya is where we are. He laughs for a long time. Then he cries for a short time. Then he sleeps.

Meter: 1,240 yen.

I have driven eleven years. I know the sound of a man who is crying and the sound of a man who is sleeping. This one did both in the wrong order. Foreigners do this.

The back seat has a broken charging port. A packet of unused wet wipes. A 2011 earthquake evacuation map. I have not taken any of them out. Passengers see them and think I am prepared. I am not prepared. I am tired.

I work nights because I do not sleep. This is simple.

---

Behind the laundromat on Maruyamacho there is music. I have driven past it nine times. Ten with tonight. Tonight I stop at the light and the passenger is asleep and the window is down two centimetres because he asked for air in a voice like a door opening.

The music comes out. Bass first. Then a voice.

Not a young voice. Not a woman's voice. A voice that goes into a room I have never entered, and follows me home into the taxi, into the rain, into the sink when I wash my hands.

*"You are allowed."*

My hand moves on the wheel. A small movement. Two centimetres. The passenger does not see. He is asleep with his mouth open, and his black sunglasses are on the seat beside him, and there is a patch on his hip where his shirt has lifted, and I do not look at it, because I have seen patches before and I know what they are and I do not ask.

I have learned this: do not ask.

The light changes. I drive.

Meter: 1,240 yen. It has said 1,240 yen for six minutes. This is because I have stopped twice and started twice and the meter does not care about stopping. It only cares about moving and not moving. This is a fair system.

---

The voice comes again. I listen. I do not say anything.

My son called me mother today.

I did not correct him. I have never corrected him.

---

The passenger wakes at 2:11. He sits up too fast and says, "Where the fuck is the door."

"I do not understand."

"The door. The — the dryer. Which one."

"I do not know."

"Four," he says. "It's four."

He reaches for the glasses on the seat and takes them. Then he gets out. He pays in cash, too much, and he says, "Keep it," and I say, "No," and he says, "Keep it, please," and I keep it. Foreign passengers tip. I do not know why. I do not ask.

He walks to the laundromat. He stands outside it for a moment with his hands in his pockets, and then he goes in, and a woman behind the counter looks up, and she does not smile, and she says something, and he nods like a man being given directions in a country where he does not know the word for left.

I watch him through the rear window until the light changes.

---

My rating is 4.8. There is one review, from three years ago, in English. It says: *driver kept crying*.

I have never told anyone I wrote that review myself. This is not true. A passenger wrote it. A woman, 2 a.m., rain, she got out at Ebisu and left an umbrella. I kept the umbrella for a year and then gave it to a man who did not need it, and the review stayed.

People think a rating is a number. It is a room. Four point eight is a room with one person in it who is crying.

---

2:58. I park outside the laundromat. Not close. Far enough that the sign is small. The music comes through the wall under the door, through the wet pavement, through the chassis of the car.

The voice says the sentence again. I listen to it the way a man listens to a meter.

A woman comes out of the laundromat and stands under the awning. Small. Grey sweatshirt. Wet hair. She looks at the dryer, not at the door, and then at my car, and then away. I know her. She is the pharmacist on the corner. She buys medicine from my wife sometimes, before my wife stopped going out.

She does not get in. I do not open the window.

At three exactly the music stops. The lights come on somewhere under the street, and the street looks the same as it did before, which is a thing I have never understood.

Meter: 1,240 yen.

The passenger is inside. He has somewhere to be for four hours. I have somewhere to be for the rest of my life.

I turn the heat up.
