# CHAPTER 1 — THE FIRST LIE

**Theo · 28 November 2024 · Narita → Shibuya**

The first lie I told Tokyo was that I had somewhere to sleep.

Not a big lie. Structurally a very small lie — the kind immigration officers file under *housing arrangements* and never think about again, the way I did not think about it, standing in Terminal 2 with £400 and a suitcase that smelled of my childhood bedroom, which is to say: of damp and Dettol and my mother's cooking, which is to say: of a building I would never enter again.

I had been in the country eleven hours. I had already lied twice. The second lie was *I'm fine*, told to a woman on the Skyliner who asked if I was alright because I was crying, and I wasn't crying, technically. My eyes were leaking. Different mechanism. British.

The truth was that my "friend's place" in Shibuya was a twenty-four-hour McDonald's on a corner, and that I had chosen it because it had outlets and no one asked you to leave if you bought a coffee every ninety minutes. I had done the maths on the plane. Four hundred pounds, eleven nights, thirty-six coffees. A person can live on coffee if they are stupid enough, and I was stupid enough, and I was also too tall and too Nigerian and too gay to sleep in a capsule hotel without the staff looking at me like I had brought a goat into a library.

I had a plan, which is a thing I have never had. The plan had three steps. Step one: find the club. Step two: be extraordinary in the club. Step three: be extraordinary somewhere else, later, in a country that had heard of me. I had written *ORIGINS* in the notes app on my phone, in capital letters, with a drawing of a microphone next to it that looked like a spoon. I had been carrying that word around for three years like a loose tooth.

The group chat was called `tokyo_afterhours` and it had 3,000 members and no profile pictures and a single rule pinned at the top, written in English by someone whose English was better than most English people's:

> *tonight's word: 雨は明日まで待てない*
> *do not translate it. say it.*

I translated it. Of course I did. *The rain cannot wait until tomorrow.* Beautiful. Useless. I said it into my phone six times, beautifully, uselessly, in the voice of a man who had been awake for twenty-two hours and smelled of Dettol. Then I listened to the playback and discovered I had said it with the intonation of a man ordering chicken. I tried again. I sounded like a man ordering a different chicken.

At 21:04 a moderator with no name deleted my attempts and posted a thumbs-up emoji. This is the most encouragement I have ever received from a stranger and I am not okay about it.

The pin dropped on 丸山町, Maruyamacho, and I stood outside WASH & GO for eleven minutes in the rain because there was no club. There was a laundromat. There was a woman behind the counter with a face like a locked door, and six dryers going, and the sound of a city that had not agreed to meet me.

I did the British thing. I stood in the rain and apologised to no one.

"Can I help you."

Not a question. The woman did not look up from a basket of socks.

"Yeah. I'm — I'm looking for Tokyo."

"The city is outside."

"Right. Ha. Yeah. No, the — the club."

Now she looked up. I have done drag for six years and I know a face that is deciding whether you are a problem. This face decided I was not a problem. This face decided I was a wanderer, which is a kind of weather.

"You sell the makeup?" she said. "Very nice. My daughter, she also like the makeup."

"I — no, I'm a —" I stopped. My suitcase was wet. My hair was doing something that VEX would have sued me for. "Yes," I said. "I sell the makeup."

"Dryer four," she said. "It sticks. You pull."

"Dryer four."

"Pull hard."

Dryer four did not stick. Dryer four opened onto a set of concrete stairs going down, and warm air came up out of them, and under the warm air was a bass line, and under the bass line was a sound I had heard in exactly one room in my life, which is to say the room I had been born in, which is to say: people being happy without asking permission.

I went down.

At the bottom was a door with no handle and a person about twenty-two with silver hair and a face like a held breath. Momo, I found out later, though he never said it and I never asked. I said the sentence. I said it wrong — three words wrong, tones like I was ordering food — and Momo did not move, did not blink, simply looked at me with the professional patience of a border.

I have been stopped at borders. I know the look. This one was better groomed.

Behind Momo, a voice shouted something in Japanese. Then, in English, with an accent I would spend the next four months learning to place and failing:

"Let him in, Momo. He can say *rain*. Any rain. Let him in."

And I, Theo Adeyemi, twenty-four, London E13, £400, eleven hours, stepped into a room that was forty-eight people too small and seventy-one people too full, and a woman at the far end of the bar pouring ginger into something that was not yet a drink looked at me the way you look at a prescription you have not decided how to read.

"Name," she said.

"Theo."

"No." She slid a glass across. "Not here."

"That's my name."

"It's a lovely name." She poured. "Drink first."

I took the glass. I did not drink. I was looking at the room, because you don't get a room like this in E13. In E13 you get a function room above a pub with a sticky floor and a smoke machine that has never been serviced, and somebody's cousin on the decks, and a fight by 1 a.m. Here there was a drag queen with a wig the size of a small dog doing lipsync in front of a wall of wet concrete, and six people in the corner laughing like they had been let out of something, and a man with cement burns on his hands standing very still near the speaker as if the bass were holding him together.

Somebody put a hand on my shoulder — small hand, cold fingers, a woman or not a woman, I could not tell and did not need to. "You okay?" they said, in English with that same syntax, the words arriving slightly out of order.

"I'm — yes. I'm fine." My mouth said it before my brain could file an objection.

"Fine is a very small word," they said, and walked away.

Behind me the DJ killed the bass and a voice came out of the speakers. An old voice. A recorded voice. A man's voice, or a voice that had been a man once, speaking English very badly, very carefully, like someone reading a sentence they had written out by hand and were afraid to drop:

*"You are allowed."*

The room did not react. The room had heard it a hundred times. The room kept dancing.

I stood there with a glass I hadn't paid for and a name I couldn't use and a voice in the wall that knew something about me that I had not yet learned.

"Who is that?" I said.

The bartender looked at the speakers. Then at me.

"Tokyo," she said.

And because I had only been in the country eleven hours, I believed her.

Later — much later, after the drink had gone warm and the queen with the dog-sized wig had finished and gone to stand in the mirror room with her face in her hands — I found the bathroom, which was a sink with a curtain, and I took off my sunglasses.

I wore them indoors. I wore them because of the patches. Not the club patches; the other ones, the ones I stick on my hip and change every Tuesday like a person paying a bill. Do not ask me why I wear a patch and also a wig and also a name that isn't on my passport. I am a very complicated man with a very simple problem, which is that I am more myself when I am not me.

Under the sink there was a mirror with a crack in it and a shelf with three things: a hairpin that looked older than me, a tube of something someone had left, and a strip of masking tape with a word written on it in marker. The word was *本音*. I did not know it then. I know it now. I know a lot of things now that I did not know at eleven hours.

I put my sunglasses back on. I looked at the crack in the mirror and did what VEX does, which is tilt the chin and decide the crack is a lighting choice.

Outside, the rain had stopped and started again, which is a thing rain does here that I am still not used to. On the street a cleaning truck went by, slowly, brushing the gutters. It was 3:06. I had nowhere to be until morning, and for the first time in my adult life that fact did not feel like a failure. It felt like a four-hour room.

I took out my phone. There was a voice note from my mother, three seconds long. She never wrote. She sent voice notes that were always three seconds long, and I always listened, and I had never once heard anything in them except breathing.

I did not send one back. I put the phone in my pocket and pulled my collar up and walked toward the McDonald's on the corner, and I did not look at the laundromat, because if you look at a locked door for too long you start to think it owes you something.

Behind me, from somewhere under the street, the bass came back on. Four hours. Then the lights. I did not know that yet either.
