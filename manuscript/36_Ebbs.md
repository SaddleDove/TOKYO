# CHAPTER 36 — EBBS

**Rin · 20 March 2026 · Shibuya**

A year is not a long time. A year is 365 pills if you take one a day, which I do not. A year is 52 Sundays, and Mei's father has called me *sister* on 31 of them, by my name on 14, and on 7 he looked at me and there was nobody behind his eyes at all.

A year is also long enough for a laundry machine to break twice.

Mrs. Saito replaced it in February. New washer, new dryer, new wall where the dryer used to be. Concrete, painted the same off-white as the rest of the room, and if you did not know, you would never know. If you did know, you stood in front of it for a while, because there is a specific grief in a wall that was once a door.

Someone had written on it.

Not spray paint. Marker. Black. Two lines, and the handwriting is nothing like mine and nothing like Mei's and nothing like Theo's, who writes like he is stabbing the page:

> *tokyo is not a place you go.*

Underneath, smaller, in a different hand, in a different ink, so it cannot have been written on the same night:

> *it's a place that goes with you.*

I do not know who wrote the second line. I have my theories and they are all selfish.

---

Here is what I know, which is not the same as what happened.

Theo sent a photograph of a form from Berlin, held up in front of a wall with a faction poster on it. *LOOK AT THE LITTLE FORM, RIN. THE LITTLE FORM IS REAL.* He performs in a club with a fire exit and 64 channels of light, and in January he sent a voice note in which he is crying and laughing at the same time, which is a VEX skill, not a Theo skill. *The lights came on at 3 and I looked for the alley and there wasn't one, so I made one.* He asked me to keep the sunglasses. They are in the drawer with the tie. I have not told Mei about the ginger. I'm telling her tomorrow.

Yusuf sends photographs of Bekasi: the road, a goat, a blue tarp, Aldi's hand on the tarp. Last week: *I am living with my husband in the same house.* I read it six times. In November: *today I say otsukaresama to the goat. She is also working.* He taught Aldi the word. Aldi uses it correctly.

Cass handed me the SD card at the sink, wrapped in a napkin from an aeroplane, with a note: *I was looking for my mother. I'm sorry I used your face to do it.* Ninety-four seconds. Neither of us has named them. In March she wrote that her hands have stopped making the shape of a camera. I wrote back: *Good.* Two letters. It was enough. It was not enough. Both.

Mei's bar on the corner of Maruyamacho is called メイ and has a licence and a fire exit and opens from 5 p.m. to midnight. Her father died on 2 November. She shaved his head that morning. She told me this once, while looking at a broken opener, of which she has forty-seven, and she would not sell the blue one, which is the whole language we speak. On the anniversary she put a piece of paper in the new bar with one word on it: *if*.

Kenji drives. In October he gave me a cassette — not the one from the flea market, a copy — with the voice on side B, between two songs, saying the sentence badly and then laughing at himself. I did not forgive him. He did not ask. We had one conversation in a parked taxi and the only part I have told anyone is this: he said *you were nine*. His son called him mother again in August. Kenji did not correct him.

---

I am twenty-nine now. I have my name. It took eleven phone calls, not twelve — I counted, because counting is what I do — and a document in triplicate and a doctor who said, kindly, *this will take time*, which is what doctors say when they mean *this will take paperwork*. The vials in my fridge now have my name on them. I still keep the old ones. I keep a lot of things past their date. The blue tie is in a drawer in my room in Shibuya, which is a thing I told myself I would never do and which I did on the second Tuesday.

Here is the ledger. Mei gave it to me on 20 March, after the last customer went, at 梅屋, with the television off and the jar of openers back on the shelf. She said: *this isn't an inheritance. it's a bill.* I have read all 214 pages. It is not money. It is: *2019 — someone slept here. 2020 — someone didn't. 2022 — a broken opener. The first one. 2023 — a man sang in the sink. Nobody knew the words. 2024 — Momo let a man in who could say rain. 2024 — Theo's first show, the old man in row three cried, do not tell him I told you. 2024 — Iqbal's boots, still by the door. 2024 — Rin came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one. 2025 — Yusuf danced. 2025.02.14 — a bottle broke. 2025.03.15 — one show. No cameras.*

The last page has lines in two hands, the first in Mei's, written on 28 November 2024:

> *someone came in.*

Under it, in a hand I know and have never asked about, there is a small addition, made in March, after the room was gone:

> *and stayed.*

I have stood in front of this new wall for six minutes. Mrs. Saito has asked me twice if I need a washing machine and I have said no twice, which is a lie, I need several.

Behind me a dryer is going. There is no secret door. I have checked. I check every time I come, which is every Tuesday, which is a thing I did not plan and have not explained to anyone.

I want to write something on the wall. I have a pen. I have had a pen for six minutes.

I put the cap back on. I do not write my name. Names are for doors, and this is a wall. The two lines are already here, in two hands, which is more than I can say for anything else that happened.

Then I go upstairs, because it is 2:58, and in two minutes a club that is not anywhere will turn its lights on, and I want to be outside for it. I want to feel it happen to the whole street. I want to stand under a sky that is not a ceiling and watch the city become, for one second, exactly what it is: a room with the lights on, full of people who are allowed.

At 3:00 the lights come on in a room with 64 channels in Neukölln, and in a bar in Oakland with a television showing birds, and in a house in Bekasi with a goat asleep under a blue tarp, and in a taxi on a wet street in Shibuya where a man has turned the heat up for a stranger.

Nobody asks anybody to leave.

Outside, a cleaning truck goes by, brushing the gutters with brushes the size of coffins. I watch it pass, the way I have watched it pass from a hundred doors, and I am no longer waiting for it to stop.

The rain waits.

It has been waiting since March. It has been waiting my whole life. It will still be waiting in November, and nobody will ever once tell it to stop, and nobody ever will.

I put the pen back in my pocket and walk home in the rain with my hands in my sweatshirt sleeves, and I am not cold, which is a fact, and not a feeling, and which I am learning to trust.
