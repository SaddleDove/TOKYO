# CHAPTER 3 — EXPIRY DATE

**Rin · 28 November 2024 · Shibuya**

The rain started in November and did not ask anyone.

I count pills for a living. Three hundred and forty-two today, I think. I stopped counting the ones I take. That is a different kind of counting.

The pharmacy is on the corner of a street that has no name in English. Morning people come in with prescriptions and complaints. Afternoon people come in with children. Night people come in with their hands over their faces, and I put the medicine in a paper bag and do not look, because looking is a thing I have learned to charge for and they have not paid.

At 4 p.m. the sun comes through the window for eleven minutes. I know this because I have measured it. After that the light goes and the fluorescent takes over, and everything in the shop becomes the colour of a hospital corridor in a film nobody wanted to see.

Here is the thing about the vials: they have a date on them. A real date. Not a feeling. Not a maybe. `2024.09`. After that, the liquid is not lying, exactly. It is just no longer telling the truth.

I keep them anyway. I keep a lot of things past their date.

There are seven of them in the box. Seven is my number. I have seven tamagoyaki pans at home, and each one has a purpose that I have never explained to anyone, and if you asked me I would lie, because the purposes are ridiculous. One is for the first roll, which is always wrong. One is for the second roll, which is always correct. One is for the days I do not eat. The others are for weather.

The label on the box says a name that is not mine. It says 佐藤 凛. It says it in the way the government says it, which is to say: without asking. I did not choose that name. I chose the sound of it once, at nine years old, in a kitchen, with my mother's back turned. I thought choosing a thing inside my head was the same as choosing it outside. It is not. Outside there are forms. Outside there are eleven phone calls, not twelve. I counted, because counting is what I do.

---

My mother called at 6:12.

"Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"Money arrived."

"Good."

"Insurance," she said. "Don't forget, the insurance."

Fifty thousand yen a month. I have sent it for four years, and she has called it insurance for four years, and she knows exactly what it is, because my mother is not a stupid woman. She is a woman who has decided that a thing cannot be said until I say it first. This is a kind of love. It is also a kind of locked room.

"Rin."

"Yes."

"It is raining there?"

"It is raining."

"Then wear a hat."

She hangs up. She does not say *I love you*. She has never said it. When I was small she said it with ginger in soup, and now she says it with the weather.

This rain, it has been falling since March. I think it knows something.

---

The scar is on the inside of my left wrist. 2016. I do not tell people the year unless they ask, and they do not ask, because I wear the sleeve of the same grey sweatshirt over it, and the sweatshirt is too big and has been too big for four years. It is the colour of a concrete wall that has been rained on.

Theo asked me once, in the alley, what the sweatshirt was.

"It's comfortable," I said.

"It's three sizes too big."

"Yes."

"So it's a room," he said. "You're wearing a room."

I did not answer. He says things like that and then he is already somewhere else, throwing the next sentence, not staying to see where the first one landed. This is not cruelty. This is what it looks like when you have never had a door closed on you from the inside.

My mother's door closed in 2016. I heard the lock. I remember the sound better than I remember her face that night.

---

At 22:40 I go behind the speakers at TOKYO with a small recorder and a cassette and a cable I have taped twice because the tape keeps coming loose.

The cassette is from a flea market in Shimokitazawa. It cost three hundred yen. No title I could read. On side B, between two songs that are both slightly out of tune, there is a man speaking English very badly, very carefully, as if he has written the sentence out by hand and is afraid of dropping it.

*"You are allowed."*

I was nine. I was under the kotatsu with my mother's brother's sake in my stomach, which I had stolen because I wanted to know what it tasted like, and that voice came out of a cheap stereo in the next room and said, in English, very badly, *you are allowed*, and then laughed at itself, a small dry laugh, and then said something in Japanese under its breath that I have played fifty times and still cannot decide whether it is a prayer or an address.

I believed it. I still do. This is either stupid or the only true thing I have.

I do not know whose voice it is. I have never tried to find out. Finding out is a kind of naming, and naming is a thing I am trying to do carefully, one form at a time, eleven phone calls, in the correct order, with the correct stamps.

I press record. I let the tape run. I take the sentence out of the cassette and put it into a file, and then into a loop, and then into the club's speakers, where it will come out at 00:17 and 01:52 and 02:41, every night, whether or not anyone needs it.

Mei says the room has heard it a hundred times.

Maybe. A room can hear a thing a hundred times and still not have it.

---

At 00:17 the voice comes on. The room keeps dancing. Theo is on the small stage in a wig the colour of a fresh plaster wall, and he is watching the speakers with his mouth slightly open, like a man who has been handed a document in a language he almost reads.

After the show he comes to the sink where I am washing a cup that is already clean.

"That voice," he says. "Who recorded it?"

"A man."

"Do you know him?"

"No."

"Then why do you play it?"

I put the cup on the rack. The water is cold. My hands are red.

"Because it's true," I said.

"Who says?"

"The man."

"The man on a tape from a flea market."

"Yes."

He laughs. Then he stops laughing, because he has heard something in my voice that he was not expecting, and Theo is not stupid either. He is loud, which people confuse with stupid.

"I'm going to do a show," he says. "Called ORIGINS. I want — I want it to mean something."

"Good."

"Will you be there?"

"I'm always there."

"Rin." He says my name like he is holding something hot. "You say things like that and then you disappear inside them."

I dry my hands on my sweatshirt. The sweatshirt has a small hole near the left pocket. I put my finger through it.

"I will be there," I say. "That is not the same as disappearing."

He wants to say more. I can see it in his face, the sentence forming, the next throw already in his hand. But the bass comes back on and someone shouts for ginger, and he goes, because Theo goes where the noise is.

I take the box of vials out of my bag and put it in the small fridge behind the sink, next to the ginger, where nobody will look. The label says my name the way the government says it. Not mine. Not yet.

Tomorrow I will call the city again. Not tomorrow. Monday. The forms are open Monday to Friday, 8:30 to 5:00, and a person has to be awake at 8:30 to use them, and I work until 2 a.m., and this is the shape of the problem.

The lights come on at three. On the way out I pass the mirror room and see Theo sitting in front of a mirror with his face in his hands, and the wig on the stool beside him, and a strip of masking tape on the wall that says 本音, which I did not put there and which I have decided not to ask about.

Outside, the rain has stopped. The street looks washed and expensive and entirely indifferent.

My feet are wet. I walk home anyway. It is not far. It is never far. That is the other problem.
