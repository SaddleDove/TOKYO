# CHAPTER 29 — GOING HOME

**Yusuf · 10 March 2025 · Shibuya**

I am going home on the twenty-third. Four days after the last night.

I have eleven months in this country and I spent nine of them on a building I will never enter. Floor thirty-one. Forty-seven floors. I put the steel where the steel goes, and the building is still standing, and I am going back to a house in Bekasi with a blue tarp over a motorbike and a goat that eats shoes.

Aldi got a message. Not a visa. A message from his mother, who says the goat is fat and the roof leaks and when is he coming home. This is how a life is decided: not with a form, but with a goat and a roof.

"I can come back," he said, over the phone. "I can apply again. In Indonesia. From there."

"Yes."

"Or you can stay."

"No."

"You want to stay?"

"I want to go home."

"Then go home." He was quiet for a bit. The goat was loud in the background. "But take the dress."

---

The dress is in a bag under my bed. I have never washed it. There is cement on the left boot still, on the dress where the boot touched it when I folded it in the mirror room that night, and I have decided not to wash it out, which is a stupid decision and which I am keeping.

On the night I danced, Theo gave me his sunglasses. Not on stage. In the mirror room, afterwards, when I was taking the dress off and he was sitting on the floor against the mirror with his face in his hands.

"These are medical," he said. "Don't tell anyone I gave you medical glasses."

"Why?"

"Because then people will think I'm generous, and I have a reputation to protect."

I said, "You are generous."

"I am a nightmare."

"Both."

He put the sunglasses in my hand and closed my fingers over them, which is a thing he does, closing people's fingers, as if a gift is a thing that needs help arriving.

In the weeks after that I kept them on the shelf in the mirror room with the old hairpin and the paper that says *also come back after*. I did not wear them. They are too dark for a room with no windows. I took them out once and held them up to the light and saw nothing through them, which is the whole idea, and which is a thing I am learning about men who hide things in plain sight.

---

At 6 p.m. I went to Rin's room. Six tatami. One bed. One fridge with seven vials in a box and a bottle of ginger that Mei had given her, which she does not drink, which I have noticed. A whole shelf of tamagoyaki pans, seven of them, and she told me the purposes, which I did not understand and which I liked.

One for the first roll, which is always wrong.
One for the second, which is always correct.
One for the days she does not eat.
The others are for weather.

She cooked on the second pan. She always cooks on the second pan now when I am there, which is a thing I have noticed and which I have decided not to mention, which is a new skill.

We ate standing up, because there is no table that is not also a bed or a desk or a place for pans.

"My flight is on the twenty-third," I said.

"Four days after."

"Four days after."

She put a piece of omelette in my hand. It was too sweet, like always.

"Will you come back?" she said.

"I don't know."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because if you say yes, you will be lying, and I don't want a lie in my room." She took a bite. "My mother is in Kobe. I have not spoken to her in four years. She sends money. I call it insurance. She calls it insurance. We are both lying and neither of us is happy."

"You should call her."

"Do not tell me what to do."

"I am asking."

She looked at me. Then she said, "Oh. You learned that."

"From Theo."

"Good student."

We ate. The window looked onto another wall. This is the whole view and it is enough. A person can live in a room with a wall for a window if the room has a door that opens, which is Mei's whole product, and which Rin's room has, and which mine in the site has never had.

At 7:20 I took the sunglasses out of my bag and put them on the table.

"These are Theo's," I said.

"I know."

"Take them."

"Why?"

"You are staying."

"For four more days."

"Four days is more than four hours." I said it slowly, in English that is almost correct, and I waited for her to correct me, and she did not. "He gave them to me and I carried them and now I am going home and I don't want to carry them on a plane. They have his patches in the case. I didn't know that until I opened it. I am not supposed to know that."

She picked them up. She held them in both hands. They are black and very dark and they make a person's face into a decision.

"Why me?" she said.

"Because you are the one who stays."

"That's not a reason."

"It is the only reason I have." I took the dress out of the bag and put it beside the glasses. "Keep this too."

"I can't keep a dress."

"Keep it for the wall."

"There is no wall here."

"Then keep it for the next wall."

---

At 23:00 I went to TOKYO. ORIGINS is on Saturday, and this is just a Monday, and there is a difference, which I know, because I have been counting.

Seventy-one people. One cat. Theo on the scaffolding with no sunglasses, which I have never seen before, and which made him look like a man who had been asked to be a person and had agreed.

He saw me look at his face.

"They're on the table at Rin's," he said.

"Yes."

"Good." Then he said, "I asked her to take them. Not you."

"I know."

"I wanted you to have them. You carried them. But you're going."

"I'm going."

"Then she keeps them." He said it in that voice he uses when he is being generous and pretending he is not, which I now recognise, which is a thing I did not recognise six months ago. "That's the whole rule, isn't it. You don't keep the thing. You pass it."

"Steel stays," I said. "People go."

"Yes." He touched my shoulder and took his hand away. "But the dress was yours."

"Tomorrow I give it back."

"No." He shook his head. "Keep the dress. Wear it on the plane."

"In Bekasi?"

"In Bekasi." He grinned. "Aldi's mother will love it."

"She will kill me."

"Then I will have done my job."

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I stood by the wall with my hands out. I have stood by this wall for four months. It has heard everything I have said in this country and it has never once repeated anything, which is a better record than any person I know.

At three the lights came on. I went home.

---

At 4 a.m. I packed. Two shirts, one pair of boots with cement on them, the dress, a photograph of my mother with a bucket, the letter with the window in it, and nothing else. A man can live with fewer things than he thinks. This is a thing I learned on floor thirty-one and have never been able to tell anyone in a way that makes them believe me.

I said *otsukaresama* to the room, which is wrong. I said it to the dress and the photograph and the letter and the wall, which is also wrong, and which I meant.

In the morning, at the airport, I will hold a boarding pass with my name on it. Yusuf Pratama. This is the name my father gave me and the name on my work visa and the name my mother shouts across the road when the goat eats her shoe. It is a true name. It is not the only true thing about me, which I have learned in this country, in a room behind a dryer, with a man in a dress who asked instead of told.

On the plane I will put the dress on my knees and I will say *otsukaresama* to the seat, which is wrong, and which I will mean.

The show is on Saturday. I will be on a plane on the twenty-third. I have asked Theo to stand by the wall for me, which he said he would do, which is a joke and also not a joke, which is his whole talent.

Theo keeps the room. Rin keeps the glasses. I keep the dress and the goat and the woman who said yes in the middle of the road in Bekasi with a goat making a noise like a small engine.

This is how a thing ends. Not with a door closing. With a thing handed to somebody else who is staying.
