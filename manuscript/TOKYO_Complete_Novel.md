# TOKYO

### A novel in three acts

> You thought you were going to Tokyo. Tokyo was the room you were standing in.

---


# ACT I — ADMISSION / 入場

---

# CHAPTER 1 — THE FIRST LIE

**Theo · 28 November 2024 · Narita → Shibuya**

The first lie I told Tokyo was that I had somewhere to sleep.

Not a big lie. Structurally a very small lie — the kind immigration officers file under *housing arrangements* and never think about again, the way I did not think about it, standing in Terminal 2 with £400 and a suitcase that smelled of my childhood bedroom, which is to say: of damp and Dettol and my mother's cooking, which is to say: of a building I would never enter again.

I had been in the country eleven hours. I had already lied twice. The second lie was *I'm fine*, told to a woman on the Skyliner who asked if I was alright because I was crying, and I wasn't crying, technically. My eyes were leaking. Different mechanism. British.

The truth was that my "friend's place" in Shibuya was a twenty-four-hour McDonald's on a corner, and that I had chosen it because it had outlets and no one asked you to leave if you bought a coffee every ninety minutes. I had done the maths on the plane. Four hundred pounds, eleven nights, thirty-six coffees. A person can live on coffee if they are stupid enough, and I was stupid enough, and I was also too tall and too Nigerian and too gay to sleep in a capsule hotel without the staff looking at me like I had brought a goat into a library.

I had a plan, which is a thing I have never had. The plan had three steps. Step one: find the club. Step two: be extraordinary in the club. Step three: be extraordinary somewhere else, later, in a country that had heard of me. I had written *ORIGINS* in the notes app on my phone, in capital letters, with a drawing of a microphone next to it that looked like a spoon. I had been carrying that word around for three years like a loose tooth.

The group chat was called `tokyo_afterhours` and it had 3,000 members and no profile pictures and a single rule pinned at the top, written in English by someone whose English was better than most English people's:

> *tonight's word: 雨は明日まで待てない*
> *do not translate it. say it.*

I translated it. Of course I did. *The rain cannot wait until tomorrow.* Beautiful. Useless. I said it into my phone six times, beautifully, uselessly, in the voice of a man who had been awake for twenty-two hours and smelled of Dettol. Then I listened to the playback and discovered I had said it with the intonation of a man ordering chicken. I tried again. I sounded like a man ordering a different chicken.

At 21:04 a moderator with no name deleted my attempts and posted a thumbs-up emoji. This is the most encouragement I have ever received from a stranger and I am not okay about it.

The pin dropped on 丸山町, Maruyamacho, and I stood outside WASH & GO for eleven minutes in the rain because there was no club. There was a laundromat. There was a woman behind the counter with a face like a locked door, and six dryers going, and the sound of a city that had not agreed to meet me.

I did the British thing. I stood in the rain and apologised to no one.

"Can I help you."

Not a question. The woman did not look up from a basket of socks.

"Yeah. I'm — I'm looking for Tokyo."

"The city is outside."

"Right. Ha. Yeah. No, the — the club."

Now she looked up. I have done drag for six years and I know a face that is deciding whether you are a problem. This face decided I was not a problem. This face decided I was a wanderer, which is a kind of weather.

"You sell the makeup?" she said. "Very nice. My daughter, she also like the makeup."

"I — no, I'm a —" I stopped. My suitcase was wet. My hair was doing something that VEX would have sued me for. "Yes," I said. "I sell the makeup."

"Dryer four," she said. "It sticks. You pull."

"Dryer four."

"Pull hard."

Dryer four did not stick. Dryer four opened onto a set of concrete stairs going down, and warm air came up out of them, and under the warm air was a bass line, and under the bass line was a sound I had heard in exactly one room in my life, which is to say the room I had been born in, which is to say: people being happy without asking permission.

I went down.

At the bottom was a door with no handle and a person about twenty-two with silver hair and a face like a held breath. Momo, I found out later, though he never said it and I never asked. I said the sentence. I said it wrong — three words wrong, tones like I was ordering food — and Momo did not move, did not blink, simply looked at me with the professional patience of a border.

I have been stopped at borders. I know the look. This one was better groomed.

Behind Momo, a voice shouted something in Japanese. Then, in English, with an accent I would spend the next four months learning to place and failing:

"Let him in, Momo. He can say *rain*. Any rain. Let him in."

And I, Theo Adeyemi, twenty-four, London E13, £400, eleven hours, stepped into a room that was forty-eight people too small and seventy-one people too full, and a woman at the far end of the bar pouring ginger into something that was not yet a drink looked at me the way you look at a prescription you have not decided how to read.

"Name," she said.

"Theo."

"No." She slid a glass across. "Not here."

"That's my name."

"It's a lovely name." She poured. "Drink first."

I took the glass. I did not drink. I was looking at the room, because you don't get a room like this in E13. In E13 you get a function room above a pub with a sticky floor and a smoke machine that has never been serviced, and somebody's cousin on the decks, and a fight by 1 a.m. Here there was a drag queen with a wig the size of a small dog doing lipsync in front of a wall of wet concrete, and six people in the corner laughing like they had been let out of something, and a man with cement burns on his hands standing very still near the speaker as if the bass were holding him together.

Somebody put a hand on my shoulder — small hand, cold fingers, a woman or not a woman, I could not tell and did not need to. "You okay?" they said, in English with that same syntax, the words arriving slightly out of order.

"I'm — yes. I'm fine." My mouth said it before my brain could file an objection.

"Fine is a very small word," they said, and walked away.

Behind me the DJ killed the bass and a voice came out of the speakers. An old voice. A recorded voice. A man's voice, or a voice that had been a man once, speaking English very badly, very carefully, like someone reading a sentence they had written out by hand and were afraid to drop:

*"You are allowed."*

The room did not react. The room had heard it a hundred times. The room kept dancing.

I stood there with a glass I hadn't paid for and a name I couldn't use and a voice in the wall that knew something about me that I had not yet learned.

"Who is that?" I said.

The bartender looked at the speakers. Then at me.

"Tokyo," she said.

And because I had only been in the country eleven hours, I believed her.

Later — much later, after the drink had gone warm and the queen with the dog-sized wig had finished and gone to stand in the mirror room with her face in her hands — I found the bathroom, which was a sink with a curtain, and I took off my sunglasses.

I wore them indoors. I wore them because of the patches. Not the club patches; the other ones, the ones I stick on my hip and change every Tuesday like a person paying a bill. Do not ask me why I wear a patch and also a wig and also a name that isn't on my passport. I am a very complicated man with a very simple problem, which is that I am more myself when I am not me.

Under the sink there was a mirror with a crack in it and a shelf with three things: a hairpin that looked older than me, a tube of something someone had left, and a strip of masking tape with a word written on it in marker. The word was *本音*. I did not know it then. I know it now. I know a lot of things now that I did not know at eleven hours.

I put my sunglasses back on. I looked at the crack in the mirror and did what VEX does, which is tilt the chin and decide the crack is a lighting choice.

Outside, the rain had stopped and started again, which is a thing rain does here that I am still not used to. On the street a cleaning truck went by, slowly, brushing the gutters. It was 3:06. I had nowhere to be until morning, and for the first time in my adult life that fact did not feel like a failure. It felt like a four-hour room.

I took out my phone. There was a voice note from my mother, three seconds long. She never wrote. She sent voice notes that were always three seconds long, and I always listened, and I had never once heard anything in them except breathing.

I did not send one back. I put the phone in my pocket and pulled my collar up and walked toward the McDonald's on the corner, and I did not look at the laundromat, because if you look at a locked door for too long you start to think it owes you something.

Behind me, from somewhere under the street, the bass came back on. Four hours. Then the lights. I did not know that yet either.

# CHAPTER 2 — LEDGER

**Mei · 28 November 2024 · 梅屋 / TOKYO**

Forty-eight people. Fire code says forty-eight. Tonight there are seventy-one and one cat, and the cat is not on the permit.

I know this because I counted. I count everything. Cups, ice, the number of times a man says *just one more* and then has three more. Money, of course. Money is the easiest thing in the world to count and the hardest thing in the world to keep.

I do not ask stories. Stories are expensive and nobody pays. I watch hands. Hands tell me everything. Theo's hands shake before a show and stop the second the mic feeds back. Rin's hands never shake. This is the problem with Rin. Somewhere in her body there is a shaking that never got permission to come out.

The new one came in at 23:20. Wet hair, borrowed rain, sunglasses on his face like he was hiding from a country that had not yet noticed him. He ordered the ginger thing before he knew what the ginger thing was. This is either confidence or hunger. With men it is usually hunger.

"Name," I said.

"Theo."

"No." I slid the glass. "Not here."

"That's my name."

"It's a lovely name." I poured. "Drink first."

He drank it like he wanted the glass to explain something to him. Then he looked at the speakers, because everyone looks at the speakers sooner or later, and he said, "Who is that?"

"Tokyo," I said.

He believed me. Of course he believed me. He had been here eleven hours and he was already in love with a room.

In eleven hours, a person can fall in love with a room. In sixteen years, a person can fall out of love with a country and still have to live in it. This is the difference between a visitor and a resident. A visitor gets to arrive.

---

The ledger is under the bar, behind the bottles nobody orders. It is a Chinese accounting book with a red cover and 214 pages and it does not contain money. Money is in the bank, where it belongs, being boring.

The ledger contains this:

*2019 — someone slept here.*
*2020 — someone didn't.*
*2022 — a broken opener. The first one.*
*2023 — a man sang in the sink. Nobody knew the words.*
*2024 — Momo let a man in who could say rain.*
*2024 — Rin came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one.*

That's the whole book. Two hundred and fourteen pages of that. People think a ledger is about debt. No. A ledger is about who showed up.

Tonight I have not written in it yet. I will, after closing. There is a line at the bottom of page 214 that is still blank, and I am saving it, which is stupid, because a blank line is not a saving. It's just a blank line.

---

My father called me sister today.

Yesterday he called me by my name. Tomorrow maybe nothing. I shave his head every Sunday. He holds still for me like he used to hold still for nothing. When he was strong he held still for nobody. He was a man who walked through a room like he owned the floor. Then the floor took him back.

I got the call at 4:20, before opening. My sister — no, not my sister, my cousin, who lives in Tokyo and has a face like an unpaid bill — said he had been sitting in the bath for two hours and would not come out because there was a woman in the house.

"Tell him I'm not a woman," I said.

"Oh, good," she said. "That will be very helpful."

I went over at five. He was in the bath, fully dressed, which is a thing he has started doing. His cardigan was floating. He looked up at me with those eyes that are still his eyes and said, in Taiwanese, "妹妹, have you eaten?"

I said, "I ate."

I did not eat. I shaved his head in the bathroom with the cardigan still on because he would not let me take it off, and he held still, and afterwards he said thank you in Japanese, which he never used to say, because Japanese was for customers.

He called me sister again on the way out. I know he means *daughter* in the way a mind that is leaving a body sometimes drops the right word on the floor and keeps walking. I know this. It does not make the word smaller.

---

Three minutes to closing. Theo is still on stage. Somebody is crying in the mirror room — somebody always is — and I put the ginger in and I don't look at the clock. The clock and I, we have an understanding: it does its job, I do mine, and at three exactly we both stop pretending.

財務大臣 is eating the garnish again.


---

At 23:41 the landlord's son came by. Not the landlord. The landlord has not come by in four years because the landlord is in Taiwan and because the landlord is my father's old friend, which is a nicer word for creditor than I would choose. The son is thirty and wears a suit that costs more than my fridge. He stood at the door of 梅屋 with an envelope in his hand and the face of a man who has been told to do something and is enjoying it more than he should.

"Ninety days," he said. "My father says ninety days."

"Your father says many things," I said. "Most of them to a lawyer."

"This is different."

"It is always different."

He put the envelope on the bar. I did not pick it up. 財務大臣 jumped onto the stool beside it and put one paw on the paper, and for one beautiful second the cat was a better lawyer than either of us.

"April first," the son said. "The whole block. They already have the permits."

"April first," I said. "Of course. Funny month for it."

He did not laugh. He was not there for laughs.

When he left, I opened the envelope. It was a notice. It was in Japanese and it was very clean and it said the word for demolition as if it were the word for lunch.

I put it in the drawer with the other notices. There are four. I have never shown any of them to anyone, not even to Rin, who would read it three times and then ask a question I could not answer in a way that would satisfy either of us.

Ninety days is a long time if you are counting forward from a door closing. It is a very short time if you are counting backward from a room that has to be emptied.

---

People ask me why I keep this place. I give them the answer with money in it because that is the answer they can hold. Rent. Debt. The loan on the bar. True, all true, none of it the reason.

The reason: in 2009 I was locked in a room for three days in Changhua and on the fourth day my aunt opened the door and said nothing, and that nothing was the only kindness I got that year. A room with a door that opens. That is the whole product. I sell doors that open.

You want a story? I got stories. They're all the same story. That's the problem.

---

02:58. Theo is still on stage, which is fine. He is still on stage at 02:58 every night because he believes the lights are a suggestion. They are not a suggestion. They are a landlord with better lighting.

The new one — Rin — is behind the speakers. She does not dance. She does not drink. She came in with wet hair and stood near the utility sink for forty minutes doing something with a small recorder and a cassette, and when I asked her what she was doing she said, "Work," in the voice of a person who has decided that the word is a locked door.

Fine. I do not ask stories.

At three exactly, the lights come on. All of them. No warning, no countdown. Seventy-one faces get ten years older in one second. Theo's glue cracks like a dry field. The queen in the corner stops mid-gesture, which is a kindness, because nobody should have to finish a gesture under fluorescent light.

People go. They always go. They go slowly, because leaving a room you love is a physical act, like pulling a plaster off a wound. The new one stays by the sink. Theo comes down from the stage and stands in the light looking like a man who has been evicted from a country, which he has not yet been.

"Four hours," he says to nobody.

"Four hours," I say, because it is true and because he needs someone to say it back.

I take the ledger out from behind the bottles. I open to page 214. The blank line is waiting. I write on it:

*someone came in.*

Then I put the ledger back. 財務大臣 sits on it.

Fine. The cat can have the last word.

# CHAPTER 3 — EXPIRY DATE

**Rin · 28 November 2024 · Shibuya**

The rain started in November and did not ask anyone.

I count pills for a living. Three hundred and forty-two today, I think. I stopped counting the ones I take. That is a different kind of counting.

The pharmacy is on the corner of a street that has no name in English. Morning people come in with prescriptions and complaints. Afternoon people come in with children. Night people come in with their hands over their faces, and I put the medicine in a paper bag and do not look, because looking is a thing I have learned to charge for and they have not paid.

At 4 p.m. the sun comes through the window for eleven minutes. I know this because I have measured it. After that the light goes and the fluorescent takes over, and everything in the shop becomes the colour of a hospital corridor in a film nobody wanted to see.

Here is the thing about the vials: they have a date on them. A real date. Not a feeling. Not a maybe. `2024.09`. After that, the liquid is not lying, exactly. It is just no longer telling the truth.

I keep them anyway. I keep a lot of things past their date.

There are seven of them in the box. Seven is my number. I have seven tamagoyaki pans at home, and each one has a purpose that I have never explained to anyone, and if you asked me I would lie, because the purposes are ridiculous. One is for the first roll, which is always wrong. One is for the second roll, which is always correct. One is for the days I do not eat. The others are for weather.

The label on the box says a name that is not mine. It says 佐藤 凛. It says it in the way the government says it, which is to say: without asking. I did not choose that name. I chose the sound of it once, at nine years old, in a kitchen, with my mother's back turned. I thought choosing a thing inside my head was the same as choosing it outside. It is not. Outside there are forms. Outside there are eleven phone calls, not twelve. I counted, because counting is what I do.

---

My mother called at 6:12.

"Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"Money arrived."

"Good."

"Insurance," she said. "Don't forget, the insurance."

Fifty thousand yen a month. I have sent it for four years, and she has called it insurance for four years, and she knows exactly what it is, because my mother is not a stupid woman. She is a woman who has decided that a thing cannot be said until I say it first. This is a kind of love. It is also a kind of locked room.

"Rin."

"Yes."

"It is raining there?"

"It is raining."

"Then wear a hat."

She hangs up. She does not say *I love you*. She has never said it. When I was small she said it with ginger in soup, and now she says it with the weather.

This rain, it has been falling since March. I think it knows something.

---

The scar is on the inside of my left wrist. 2016. I do not tell people the year unless they ask, and they do not ask, because I wear the sleeve of the same grey sweatshirt over it, and the sweatshirt is too big and has been too big for four years. It is the colour of a concrete wall that has been rained on.

Theo asked me once, in the alley, what the sweatshirt was.

"It's comfortable," I said.

"It's three sizes too big."

"Yes."

"So it's a room," he said. "You're wearing a room."

I did not answer. He says things like that and then he is already somewhere else, throwing the next sentence, not staying to see where the first one landed. This is not cruelty. This is what it looks like when you have never had a door closed on you from the inside.

My mother's door closed in 2016. I heard the lock. I remember the sound better than I remember her face that night.

---

At 22:40 I go behind the speakers at TOKYO with a small recorder and a cassette and a cable I have taped twice because the tape keeps coming loose.

The cassette is from a flea market in Shimokitazawa. It cost three hundred yen. No title I could read. On side B, between two songs that are both slightly out of tune, there is a man speaking English very badly, very carefully, as if he has written the sentence out by hand and is afraid of dropping it.

*"You are allowed."*

I was nine. I was under the kotatsu with my mother's brother's sake in my stomach, which I had stolen because I wanted to know what it tasted like, and that voice came out of a cheap stereo in the next room and said, in English, very badly, *you are allowed*, and then laughed at itself, a small dry laugh, and then said something in Japanese under its breath that I have played fifty times and still cannot decide whether it is a prayer or an address.

I believed it. I still do. This is either stupid or the only true thing I have.

I do not know whose voice it is. I have never tried to find out. Finding out is a kind of naming, and naming is a thing I am trying to do carefully, one form at a time, eleven phone calls, in the correct order, with the correct stamps.

I press record. I let the tape run. I take the sentence out of the cassette and put it into a file, and then into a loop, and then into the club's speakers, where it will come out at 00:17 and 01:52 and 02:41, every night, whether or not anyone needs it.

Mei says the room has heard it a hundred times.

Maybe. A room can hear a thing a hundred times and still not have it.

---

At 00:17 the voice comes on. The room keeps dancing. Theo is on the small stage in a wig the colour of a fresh plaster wall, and he is watching the speakers with his mouth slightly open, like a man who has been handed a document in a language he almost reads.

After the show he comes to the sink where I am washing a cup that is already clean.

"That voice," he says. "Who recorded it?"

"A man."

"Do you know him?"

"No."

"Then why do you play it?"

I put the cup on the rack. The water is cold. My hands are red.

"Because it's true," I said.

"Who says?"

"The man."

"The man on a tape from a flea market."

"Yes."

He laughs. Then he stops laughing, because he has heard something in my voice that he was not expecting, and Theo is not stupid either. He is loud, which people confuse with stupid.

"I'm going to do a show," he says. "Called ORIGINS. I want — I want it to mean something."

"Good."

"Will you be there?"

"I'm always there."

"Rin." He says my name like he is holding something hot. "You say things like that and then you disappear inside them."

I dry my hands on my sweatshirt. The sweatshirt has a small hole near the left pocket. I put my finger through it.

"I will be there," I say. "That is not the same as disappearing."

He wants to say more. I can see it in his face, the sentence forming, the next throw already in his hand. But the bass comes back on and someone shouts for ginger, and he goes, because Theo goes where the noise is.

I take the box of vials out of my bag and put it in the small fridge behind the sink, next to the ginger, where nobody will look. The label says my name the way the government says it. Not mine. Not yet.

Tomorrow I will call the city again. Not tomorrow. Monday. The forms are open Monday to Friday, 8:30 to 5:00, and a person has to be awake at 8:30 to use them, and I work until 2 a.m., and this is the shape of the problem.

The lights come on at three. On the way out I pass the mirror room and see Theo sitting in front of a mirror with his face in his hands, and the wig on the stool beside him, and a strip of masking tape on the wall that says 本音, which I did not put there and which I have decided not to ask about.

Outside, the rain has stopped. The street looks washed and expensive and entirely indifferent.

My feet are wet. I walk home anyway. It is not far. It is never far. That is the other problem.

# CHAPTER 4 — FORTY MINUTES

**Yusuf · 29 November 2024 · Shibuya construction site → TOKYO**

I am in Japan for making buildings. Forty-seven floors. I am on floor thirty-one and I put the steel where the steel goes. This I understand. Steel has a place. You put it there or the building falls.

Down I do not look. Up I look and I am afraid. This is backwards, Iqbal said. Iqbal is from Makassar and he is afraid of nothing, even his wife. He says this and everybody laughs because his wife is a small person and Iqbal is not. Then he crosses himself, which is a thing he does even though he is Muslim, and he says it is for luck, and my mother would not like this but my mother is not here.

My mother is in Java. My brother is in Java. Aldi is in Bekasi and it is raining there too and we talk about the rain because the other things need more than forty minutes.

Every night with Aldi, forty minutes. The screen is small. I hold the phone with my left hand because the right hand has cement on it that does not come off. Iqbal says I should wash. I say I wash. He says, no, you *wash*. He has a way of saying English words that makes them sound like tools.

"Kangen," Aldi says. I miss you.

I say it back. Then the signal finishes and I am holding a phone like holding a cup with no coffee.

I have two hundred Japanese words. I use the same six every day. *Otsukaresama* means thank you for working, I think. Or it means you are tired. I say it to the security man at the gate and he says it back and we are both correct. Yesterday I said it to a photograph of my mother that I keep in my wallet. This is wrong, I think. But she is tired.

---

The accident is not tonight. Tonight is a normal night. Tonight we work until six and then the foreman says something in Japanese and points at the sky, which means the wind is coming, which means tomorrow we do not work on the outside.

I like the wind less than the height. The height is a fact. A fact you can measure. The wind is a person touching you when you did not say yes.

At 19:00 we change clothes. Iqbal puts on a shirt that is too clean for a construction site and says he is going to see a woman. I say, "Which woman." He says, "A woman who will marry me, maybe." I say, "You already have a wife." He says, "This is why I say maybe."

We laugh. We are twenty floors up in our minds and three thousand kilometres from our mothers and we laugh like men who are not afraid.

Then Iqbal says, "You come with me."

"I cannot."

"You come. You stand outside. You do not have to do anything."

"I cannot."

He puts his hand on my shoulder. His hand is heavier than a tool. "You are always cannot. Cannot drink. Cannot dance. Cannot tell Aldi. You make a cage in your head and then you live in it."

I say, "I am living in Japan for making buildings. I did not know I am also making a cage."

He does not understand. Or he understands and he is angry. Both are possible. He takes his hand away and goes.

---

The bar is a laundry machine.

This is the truth and I will say it exactly: the entrance to the club is a dryer in a laundry shop. A woman with a face like a locked gate sits behind the counter. I do not know her name. She looks at me and says, "You sell the makeup?"

"No," I say. "I am for the steel."

She looks at my hands. Then she says, "Dryer four. Pull."

I pull. There are stairs. There is music under the stairs. There is warm air, and the warm air smells like ginger and sweat and something old, like a closet in a house where somebody has been crying.

At the bottom a person with silver hair holds out one hand. I know this is a door. I have built doors. You put the steel where the steel goes, and then a person can pass.

The word tonight is about rain. I practice it in the taxi on the way. I say it wrong in the taxi. The driver listens in the mirror and says something in Japanese and I think he is helping me and I say thank you and he says something else and we both smile. Later I find out he said, *the rain is not your friend*. Or maybe he said, *the rain is not your fault*. My Japanese is two hundred words and all of them are almost correct.

I say the sentence to the silver-haired person. I say it wrong. He looks at me for a long time. Behind him a woman's voice shouts, and then says in English: "Let him in. Any rain. Let him in."

---

Inside, there is a room with no windows and too many people. I have been on floor thirty-one of a building with no walls yet and this room is more dangerous. A building wants to stand. A room like this does not want anything. It just wants you to stay until three, and then it wants you gone.

Iqbal is somewhere. I cannot see him. I go to the wall because the wall is a thing I understand, and I stand with my back against it.

A man in a dress comes over. He is very tall and his face is not a face, it is a decision. He puts a glass in my hand.

"Darling," he says. "Are you here or are you here?"

"I don't understand."

"Good. Better."

He laughs and goes. The glass in my hand is cold. I drink because that is what you do with a glass.

Then a small person with cold fingers touches my arm and says, "You want to see the mirror room?"

I follow. The mirror room has six mirrors and a table with glue and powder and a hairpin that looks very old. On the wall there are papers. Small pieces of paper with words on them. I can read four of them.

*You look good today.*
*Don't be afraid.*
*Come back before three.*

I stand in front of the mirror for twenty minutes. I do not move. I am not beautiful. I am a man from Java with cement on his hands and two hundred words and a shirt I bought at a station. But the mirrors do not ask me to be beautiful. They just hold me. This is a new thing. A mirror that does not ask.

Outside, a woman is singing. It is not a Japanese song. It is a song I know from a bus in Bekasi, from a karaoke place with a broken air conditioner, from my sister's wedding. I start to sing it under my breath. I get the words wrong in English. I get them right in my head.

I stand there with my hands up because I have seen other people do this and nobody has told me to stop.

# CHAPTER 5 — VEX

**Theo · 6 December 2024 · TOKYO**

# CHAPTER 6 — EXTRACTIVE

**Cass · 12 December 2024 · TOKYO**

I should say at the outset that I am aware of what this project is. It is extractive. I know the literature — I can cite four scholars who would describe my methodology as a form of polite violence, and I would agree with all of them, and then I would keep shooting, because the thing about critique is that it is a very comfortable place to live and the footage is still there to be shot.

I am twenty-six — twenty-six — and I have a grant from a foundation whose board I will not name, and I came to Tokyo to find "authentic queer interiority," which is a phrase I wrote at 2 a.m. and have been ashamed of every day since.

Let me be more precise, because precision is the last available virtue. I am not here because I love Tokyo. I am here because I have spent twenty-six years constructing a documentary about a woman I have never met, and at some point the word *mother* became too small to put in a grant application, so I wrote *Tokyo* instead, and then *diaspora*, and then *interiority*, and by the time the cheque arrived I had successfully buried the actual sentence under six layers of academic language, which is what academic language is for.

This is not a confession. Confession is a genre with a redemption arc and I am not eligible.

---

The club is behind a dryer. I want to write that sentence in a way that would make an editor at a festival nod slowly — *behind the domestic machine, the other room* — but the truth is more embarrassing and more interesting: it is a dryer in a laundromat and you pull it and there are stairs. I pulled it four times before I got the angle I wanted. Mrs. Saito, who runs the place, watched me do this and said something in Japanese and then, in English, "You make film? My daughter makes the TikTok."

"Yes," I said. "Very similar."

It was not similar. It was the opposite. But I have found that if you say *yes* to people in this city, doors open, and if you say *actually*, they close. This is a small piece of honne/tatemae I have learned without being able to use the words correctly, which is my whole problem here in miniature.

There is a person at the bottom of the stairs whose name I learned later was Momo. They held out a hand. I said the password wrong. They looked at me. I have been looked at like that by border officials in fourteen countries and by one ex-girlfriend in Oakland, and in every case the message was identical: *you may think you have a right to be here; I am paid to disagree.*

A woman's voice from behind them said, in English, "She can say rain. Let her in."

So I was let in, which is a thing that happens to you when someone else decides you are a person. I have built an entire career on not noticing that this is what happens.

---

Inside: a room that is too full and too warm, wet concrete, a scaffolding stage, a bar made of plumbing. The light is pink. I want to say the light is *locally legible* but it is pink. There is an older woman behind the bar who watches hands, not faces, and a pharmacist who does not dance, and a very tall drag queen with the kind of cheekbones that make you angry about your own face.

I put the camera up immediately. That is my method. You put the camera up first, so that everyone knows the camera is there, so that what follows cannot be called a surprise. This is not true. It is a thing I tell myself in the shower.

The first person I filmed was the pharmacist. She was standing near the sink with a small recorder in her hand, doing something with a cassette. Her hair was wet. Her sweatshirt was too big. In the viewfinder she became an arrangement of textures: wet hair, grey cotton, the red of her knuckles, an object with a label I could not read.

I filmed her for three minutes and eleven seconds.

Then she turned and looked into the lens.

Not into me. Into the lens. This is a specific and terrible thing. People who are used to cameras look into the lens because they know the lens is a machine and machines can be negotiated with. People who are not used to cameras look into the lens because they have understood, too late, that the machine is a person with a story to tell about them.

She walked over. She did not hurry. I admire that about her now; I did not admire it then. I was still in the part of the encounter where I was thinking about framing.

"Delete it," she said.

Her English is better than mine and quieter. This is another thing I have since had to think about.

I said, "I'm documenting — "

"Delete it."

"I will." This is the sentence I am least proud of. I said it in the voice I use for airport staff and foundation board members: warm, efficient, already leaving. "Of course. I'll delete it."

I did not delete it.

I want to be exact about this, because exactness is the only thing I have left. I reached for the camera, and my hand went to the wrong button — the display button, not delete — and then I thought about doing it properly and decided not to, because the footage was good. That is the whole moral architecture of the event. One button. One decision. A very small physical movement that I have replayed more often than any footage.

She watched my hand move. Then she walked away.

I have a theory that the body keeps a ledger and the mind is only the bookkeeper, and the bookkeeper is always the last to know.

---

At 2:40 I pointed the camera at the drag queen, who was applying glue in the mirror room with the concentration of a surgeon. She looked at the lens, then at me, and said, "You are the problem, yeah?"

"I'm documenting —"

"Yes. You are the problem. But you are *my* problem, so —" she gestured at the mirrors, at the six of them, at the strips of paper on the wall — "you can stay. Don't point that at Rin again."

"Rin."

"The pharmacist. She has a name. You should use it."

I stopped filming. I have thought about this instruction more than any note I have ever received from a subject. It was not a request for privacy. It was a correction of *method*. She was telling me that the thing I called *authentic interiority* had a first name and a job and a habit of standing near sinks.

I put the camera down. Then I picked it up again, because I am twenty-six and I have a grant and a very large, very comfortable word for what I am doing, which is *process*.

The camera chose that moment to die. Not dramatically. No flicker. Just a small black screen where the face had been.

I have never been so grateful for a piece of plastic in my life.

---

On the street at 3:14, the cleaning truck going by, the rain starting again in the way rain starts here — not falling so much as being decided — I took out the SD card and held it in my hand. It is a small grey rectangle. It weighs nothing. It contains forty-one minutes of people who did not agree to be in my film, and one selfie I took in the mirror at the bar, which I have tried to delete four times and cannot, because some part of me insists on being in the footage too.

This is the sentence I would put in the grant report if I were honest, which I am not: *I wanted to find a woman who left, and instead I found a room that closes at three, and I filmed it as if closure were a metaphor, when it was only a rule.*

Later I will tell myself that I was tired. That I was jet-lagged. That the battery died for a reason. These are all true and none of them are the reason.

The reason is that the footage was good.

I am going to keep the card in my pocket for the next four months. I am going to tell myself that keeping it is different from using it. I am going to be very careful with the word *different*.

# CHAPTER 7 — HONNE

**Rin · 19 December 2024 · Shibuya**

"I ate."

"Eleven is not a meal. Eleven is a snack with ambition."

"Say what."

"The thing. The sentence you have in your mouth. You have had it since Tuesday."

"I sent her a voice note tonight," he said. "Three seconds. Breathing. I can't say *I am going to be someone you can love.* I can only breathe at her."

"So say it."

"If I say it, it becomes a thing."

"Yes."

"Then she has to answer."

"Yes."

"And if she answers wrong —"

"Then you know." He said it without drama. Theo almost never says things without drama. This is why I believed him.

"Is that the tape on the wall?"

"Yes."

"What does it mean, really. Not the dictionary."

"Tatemae is the room."

"Yes."

"Yes."

"Yes."

"So maybe the true thing can be said in the street."

I have never liked this about him. He takes a word and turns it until it becomes a tool. It is very English. It is very American. It is also, occasionally, kind.

"I want to change one character," I said. "On my family register. One. To make the whole line true. To make my mother's money insurance and my father's silence something else. I called twelve times. No — eleven. I counted. They say come in person, then the form is closed, then come with a doctor's letter, then the doctor says *this will take time*, which means *this will take paperwork*. In eleven phone calls there is no sentence for *I am a woman*."

Theo did not say anything. This is a skill I did not know he had.

Then he said, "You can change your name. I can change my whole life and still not change my visa."

"Different."

"Same." He touched his lip. "You know what is funny. In London I used to shout that I was real. *Look at me, I'm here, I'm real.* Here nobody shouts. Here everyone is real in a basement for four hours and then goes home to a family register that says something else."

"A person can be real in four hours."

"A person can be real in four minutes." He put the second rice ball in my hand. "I just want more than four."

"I want one person to know," I said. "Only one. Not my mother. Not yet. One."

"And?"

"I don't know who."

"Oh," he said. "Right. Of course."

He said it in such a small voice that I looked up. He was smiling, but it was not a joke-smile. It was the smile people use when they have been given a seat on a bus and are about to stand up again.

"With the show?"

"Okay."

"I won't."

"And don't say *I'm here* like you're throwing it."

"Okay," he said. "I'll say it quietly."

"Rain," he said, in Japanese, badly.

"Rain," I said, correctly.

He grinned. "See? We're learning each other's languages."

I did not correct him. We were not. We were learning each other's silences, which is harder, and which nobody has made a textbook for.

# CHAPTER 8 — THE LEDGER, CONTINUED

# CHAPTER 9 — CONCRETE

**Yusuf · 27 December 2024 · Shibuya construction site**

The building is forty-seven floors. I am on thirty-one and I put the steel where the steel goes. I know where it goes. I have put it there nine hundred times.

Down I do not look. Up I look and I am afraid. Iqbal says this is backwards. "My heart is everywhere. This is my problem."

# CHAPTER 14 — READ RECEIPT

# CHAPTER 16 — STRONGER NARRATIVE

**Cass · 3 February 2025 · TOKYO**

# CHAPTER 28 — NINETY-FOUR SECONDS

**Cass · 2 March 2025 · Shibuya**

I put my finger on the delete button. Then I took it off. Then I put it on again.

This is the whole moral architecture of my life, repeated in private, with no witness, which is worse than doing it in front of someone, because there is nobody to stop me and nobody to forgive me.

I have a theory about why I could not delete it. The theory is this: I did not want to keep *her*. The second theory is worse and shorter: I wanted the footage.

I am aware of the literature. I can cite four scholars. I can cite myself. This is the problem with self-awareness: it is a very comfortable place to live and the footage is still there to be deleted.

There was no click. This is the truth I would put in the grant report if I were honest, which I am not.

Then I deleted everything else.

Yusuf by the wall, hands out of pockets, four minutes and eight seconds. Theo on the scaffolding with a broom, ninety seconds of a joke about a form which was not a joke. Ninety-four seconds. I have not shown it to anyone. I will not show it to Rin. It is not different. It was never different. This is the sentence I have been walking toward for four months and I have finally arrived, and there is no room at the end of it, just a desk lamp and a leak in the ceiling and a plastic cup of water on the floor.

Two men in hard hats went into WASH & GO first. They took out the dryers. Dryer four came out at 8:41, which I know because I looked at my phone, and because counting is what I do. They carried it to a truck and put it on top of a pile of other machines, and it looked small up there, which was strange, because it was a door.

Then they took the wall.

Not all of it. They took a section behind where the dryer had been, which is the place where the stairs went down, and they worked with a saw that made a sound like a person shouting in a language I could not understand, and concrete dust came out and went into the street and got on the shoes of a woman waiting for the bus, who brushed it off without looking, because in this city people brush things off without looking, which is a skill I have spent four months learning and will now never need.

At 9:20 a man came out of the building carrying a piece of the sign. The light box from the old pachinko hall, with half of a character missing. He put it in the truck. I wanted to take it. I did not take it. A thing you take becomes a thing you have to explain, and I have spent my whole life not explaining, and I am tired, and I am still not taking it.

At 10:02 the stairs were gone. There was a flat wall where they had been, the colour of the rest of the room, and a man was sweeping the dust into a pile.

This is the whole demolition. Two hours and forty-one minutes. I had imagined it would take longer, or be louder, or that someone would come out, which nobody did, because there was nobody in there, because the room had emptied itself at three the night before and had not taken anything with it.

Mei was at 梅屋 at 3 p.m. with three customers, one hiding from his wife, one asleep, one new, who asked about the jar of forty-seven broken openers and whether they were for sale.

"No," Mei said.

"I'll give you five thousand for one."

"Which one?"

"The blue one."

"That one has the best personality. Ten thousand."

The man laughed. Mei did not. She was wearing a black dress under her apron, which I had never seen her wear, and which she did not explain, and which she will never admit to, the way Theo will never admit about the wig.

At 5:30 the last customer went. She turned the television off, which was showing baseball in March, and she took the ledger out of her bag and put it on the bar in front of me.

"This isn't an inheritance," she said. "It's a bill."

I opened it. Two hundred and fourteen pages. I have read four of them, and I will read the rest, and I will not read them tonight, because tonight is for leaving, and reading is for staying.

"I can't take this," I said.

"You can't not take it." She put a glass down. "Drink first."

"Mei."

"Drink first. Then argue."

I drank. Ginger. Everything here is ginger. 

"You should call your mother," she said.

"Do not tell me what to do."

"I am asking." She wiped the counter, which was clean. "I ask because I am leaving tomorrow and I will not be here to ask again. My father is in a hospital in Nakano. He called me sister yesterday and by my name the day before and neither of those is me, but I shave his head anyway, because that is a thing I can do, and it is a thing he lets me do, and that is the whole marriage, and the whole disease, and the whole daughter."

I put my hand on the ledger. The cover was red and the corners were soft from eleven years of hands.

"Why me?" I said.

"Because you came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one."

"That's in the book."

"Yes. I wrote it. You should not read your own line. It's embarrassing."

"Are you happy?"

"No." She poured another glass. "Both. I am alive, which is not a small thing to write. Ask me again in a year."

At 6 p.m. I went home. My room has six tatami and a wall for a window and a shelf of seven pans and a box of vials in the fridge with my name on them now, which is a sentence I have said to no one and which I will say to one person someday, and I do not know who, and I have stopped caring that I do not know who.

On the table: the recorder, the cassette, the screwdriver, and a blue tie that I took from a man who held it out. I have not worn it. I have not put it in a drawer either, which is a thing I would have done a year ago.

There is a paper bag on the table. It was not there this morning. Theo must have left it before dawn, because he cannot give a thing directly and keep his face straight. Inside is a strip of masking tape with STAY written in my own hand and a pair of black sunglasses, cleaned of patches and folded neatly, with no note.

I put them on. They are too dark for a room with a wall for a window. I took them off. I put them on again. I did the thing four times in thirty seconds and called it a creative process, which is a sentence I learned from Theo, who learned it from nobody, who made it up in a laundromat at 3:06 a.m., which is the kind of thing a person makes up in a laundromat at 3:06 a.m.

Outside, the sky was the colour of a washed glass. The light was the same length as the dark, which is a fact and not a feeling, and which I am going to hold onto, because facts are the only things I trust now, and I have very few of them.

I called my mother.

It was 7 p.m. in Shibuya and 6 p.m. in Kobe. She answered on the second ring.

"Did you eat?" she said.

"I ate."

"Don't lie to your mother."

"I'm not lying to my mother."

A pause. A long one. The kind of pause that has four years in it and fifty thousand yen a month and a word neither of us has ever said in the right order.

"Rin," she said.

"Yes."

I sat down on the floor with the phone against my ear and the ledger on the table in front of me and the sunglasses in my hand and the vial box in the fridge with my name on it, and I said the word I have had in my mouth for four years, which is not a big word, which is a small word, which is the smallest word that is still big enough.

I said: "I am your daughter."

She did not answer right away. Then she said, "Okay."

One word. Not a poem. Not a forgiveness. A word.

Okay.

It was enough. It was not enough. Both, which is the only honest answer I have ever given anyone, and which I gave to a woman who asked me if I was happy, and which I gave to a taxi driver who said *you were nine*, and which I gave to a room that is now a wall.

At 8 p.m. the lights came on somewhere in this city. There is no club. There is no alley. There is no door behind a dryer. But a room with the lights on is a room with the lights on, and I am standing in one, and there is nobody here to tell me to leave, and I am not going to.

The rain waited.

It always waits.

# CHAPTER 36 — EBBS

**Rin · 20 March 2026 · Shibuya**

A year is not a long time. A year is 365 pills if you take one a day, which I do not. A year is 52 Sundays, and Mei's father has called me *sister* on 31 of them, by my name on 14, and on 7 he looked at me and there was nobody behind his eyes at all.

A year is also long enough for a laundry machine to break twice.

Mrs. Saito replaced it in February. New washer, new dryer, new wall where the dryer used to be. Concrete, painted the same off-white as the rest of the room, and if you did not know, you would never know. If you did know, you stood in front of it for a while, because there is a specific grief in a wall that was once a door.

Someone had written on it.

Not spray paint. Marker. Black. Two lines, and the handwriting is nothing like mine and nothing like Mei's and nothing like Theo's, who writes like he is stabbing the page:

> *tokyo is not a place you go.*

Underneath, smaller, in a different hand, in a different ink, so it cannot have been written on the same night:

> *it's a place that goes with you.*

I do not know who wrote the second line. I have my theories and they are all selfish.

---

Here is what I know, which is not the same as what happened.

Theo sent a photograph of a form from Berlin, held up in front of a wall with a faction poster on it. *LOOK AT THE LITTLE FORM, RIN. THE LITTLE FORM IS REAL.* He performs in a club with a fire exit and 64 channels of light, and in January he sent a voice note in which he is crying and laughing at the same time, which is a VEX skill, not a Theo skill. *The lights came on at 3 and I looked for the alley and there wasn't one, so I made one.* He asked me to keep the sunglasses. They are in the drawer with the tie. I have not told Mei about the ginger. I'm telling her tomorrow.

Yusuf sends photographs of Bekasi: the road, a goat, a blue tarp, Aldi's hand on the tarp. Last week: *I am living with my husband in the same house.* I read it six times. In November: *today I say otsukaresama to the goat. She is also working.* He taught Aldi the word. Aldi uses it correctly.

Cass handed me the SD card at the sink, wrapped in a napkin from an aeroplane, with a note: *I was looking for my mother. I'm sorry I used your face to do it.* Ninety-four seconds. Neither of us has named them. In March she wrote that her hands have stopped making the shape of a camera. I wrote back: *Good.* Two letters. It was enough. It was not enough. Both.

Mei's bar on the corner of Maruyamacho is called メイ and has a licence and a fire exit and opens from 5 p.m. to midnight. Her father died on 2 November. She shaved his head that morning. She told me this once, while looking at a broken opener, of which she has forty-seven, and she would not sell the blue one, which is the whole language we speak. On the anniversary she put a piece of paper in the new bar with one word on it: *if*.

Kenji drives. In October he gave me a cassette — not the one from the flea market, a copy — with the voice on side B, between two songs, saying the sentence badly and then laughing at himself. I did not forgive him. He did not ask. We had one conversation in a parked taxi and the only part I have told anyone is this: he said *you were nine*. His son called him mother again in August. Kenji did not correct him.

I am twenty-nine now. I have my name. It took eleven phone calls, not twelve — I counted, because counting is what I do — and a document in triplicate and a doctor who said, kindly, *this will take time*, which is what doctors say when they mean *this will take paperwork*. The vials in my fridge now have my name on them. I still keep the old ones. I keep a lot of things past their date. The blue tie is in a drawer in my room in Shibuya, which is a thing I told myself I would never do and which I did on the second Tuesday.

Here is the ledger. Mei gave it to me on 20 March, after the last customer went, at 梅屋, with the television off and the jar of openers back on the shelf. She said: *this isn't an inheritance. it's a bill.* I have read all 214 pages. It is not money. It is: *2019 — someone slept here. 2020 — someone didn't. 2022 — a broken opener. The first one. 2023 — a man sang in the sink. Nobody knew the words. 2024 — Momo let a man in who could say rain. 2024 — Theo's first show, the old man in row three cried, do not tell him I told you. 2024 — Iqbal's boots, still by the door. 2024 — Rin came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one. 2025 — Yusuf danced. 2025.02.14 — a bottle broke. 2025.03.15 — one show. No cameras.*

The last page has lines in two hands, the first in Mei's, written on 28 November 2024:

> *someone came in.*

Under it, in a hand I know and have never asked about, there is a small addition, made in March, after the room was gone:

> *and stayed.*

I have stood in front of this new wall for six minutes. Mrs. Saito has asked me twice if I need a washing machine and I have said no twice, which is a lie, I need several.

Behind me a dryer is going. There is no secret door. I have checked. I check every time I come, which is every Tuesday, which is a thing I did not plan and have not explained to anyone.

I want to write something on the wall. I have a pen. I have had a pen for six minutes.

I put the cap back on. I do not write my name. Names are for doors, and this is a wall. The two lines are already here, in two hands, which is more than I can say for anything else that happened.

Then I go upstairs, because it is 2:58, and in two minutes a club that is not anywhere will turn its lights on, and I want to be outside for it. I want to feel it happen to the whole street. I want to stand under a sky that is not a ceiling and watch the city become, for one second, exactly what it is: a room with the lights on, full of people who are allowed.

At 3:00 the lights come on in a room with 64 channels in Neukölln, and in a bar in Oakland with a television showing birds, and in a house in Bekasi with a goat asleep under a blue tarp, and in a taxi on a wet street in Shibuya where a man has turned the heat up for a stranger.

Nobody asks anybody to leave.

Outside, a cleaning truck goes by, brushing the gutters with brushes the size of coffins. I watch it pass, the way I have watched it pass from a hundred doors, and I am no longer waiting for it to stop.

The rain waits.

It has been waiting since March. It has been waiting my whole life. It will still be waiting in November, and nobody will ever once tell it to stop, and nobody ever will.

I put the pen back in my pocket and walk home in the rain with my hands in my sweatshirt sleeves, and I am not cold, which is a fact, and not a feeling, and which I am learning to trust.
