# CHAPTER 30 — TOKYO

**Kenji · 12 March 2025 · Shibuya**

Rain. Wednesday. 2:58 a.m.

I park. The meter runs. 1,180 yen.

A woman comes out of WASH & GO. Grey sweatshirt. Wet hair. Too-big sleeves. Sun glasses in her hand. Black. I have seen them before. On a man in my back seat. On Christmas.

She opens the back door. She gets in.

"Shibuya," she says.

"We are in Shibuya."

"Then drive."

I drive.

She holds a small recorder. Black. Tape. A cable taped with white tape. She works at the pharmacy. I know this. She buys nothing. She comes in sometimes with wet hair.

The recorder slips. It falls on the seat. The cassette comes out.

She says, "Ah."

I stop at the light. I pick up the cassette.

It is small. Brown. A label on side B. Blue ink. My hand. My writing. I know my writing. Twenty-five years do not change a man's writing.

The label says one word.

TOKYO.

Under it, a date. 1999.03.

I hold the cassette.

The light changes. A car behind me beeps. I drive.

---

1999. I was twenty-six.

A room under a pachinko hall in Shinjuku. Sign with half a character missing. Forty people. Maybe fifty. Music too loud. A thing made of pipe. Two metres high.

I went up.

I stood up there. A man. One night.

A woman with a wig held my hand before I went up. She said in English, badly, *you are allowed*. I laughed at the English. I said it back. Then I said something in Japanese under my breath.

She recorded it. On a cassette. She gave me a copy. I put the label on myself. Blue ink. My hand.

In March the building burned. The man on the pipe burned with it.

In October I married a woman. I was good at it. I was good at being her husband. I was good at being his father.

She knew. From the third year. She never said.

I never said.

The cassette went into a box. The box went into a drawer. The drawer went into a room that was not mine. Then a flea market. Then a girl of nine under a kotatsu. Then a woman in a grey sweatshirt with a recorder and a cable taped with white tape.

I hold the cassette.

The wipers go. 1,240 yen.

---

"Sir," the woman says. "The cassette."

I give it back.

This is an action. It is the only action.

She takes it. She puts it in the recorder. She presses one button. Then another. This is a thing she does twice. Once to do the thing. Once to make sure.

The tape hisses.

Then the voice.

*"You are allowed."*

In my back seat. In a taxi. In the rain. Twenty-five years. My voice. Bad English. Careful. Then the small dry laugh. Then the Japanese under the breath.

The woman watches the recorder. She does not look at the mirror. She does not look at me.

I drive.

1,240 yen. 1,240. 1,240.

She says, "You know it?"

"No."

"I found it in Shimokitazawa. Three hundred yen. No title I could read."

"Good price."

"Yes."

She puts the recorder in her bag. She holds the bag on her knees. She does not know. I do not tell her.

I do not tell her.

---

At 3:04 we stop outside a building with another wall for a window.

"Here," she says.

I do not move.

The meter says 1,240 yen. It said 1,240 yen before. I have driven nine minutes and the meter says 1,240 yen. This is wrong. The meter does not care. It only cares about moving and not moving.

"Here," she says again.

I put the hand brake on. I turn. I look at her.

She has the sunglasses in her hand. Black. Theo's. She puts them on the seat beside her. She leaves them.

"Keep them," she says.

"I do not keep things."

"Then throw them away."

"I do not throw things away."

"Then keep them."

She gets out. She shuts the door. She stands on the pavement in the rain with her hand on the bag, and the bag holds the recorder and the tape and the voice.

I watch her in the mirror. She walks to the door. She does not look back.

At 3:06 the door closes.

---

I do not ask where she takes the tape. I do not ask what room she puts it in. A man who drives at night learns not to ask.

I know a label. Blue ink. My hand. TOKYO. 1999.03.

I know a man who stood on a pipe for one night.

I know a woman who held his hand and said a sentence badly.

I know that sentence came back to me in the back of my own car and I gave it back to the person who carried it, and I said nothing, and this is the thing I do. This is the whole thing I have done for twenty-five years.

I say nothing. I have practiced.

---

The sunglasses are on the back seat. Black. Medical. The man in the back seat that night was foreign and drunk and said Shibuya when we were in Shibuya. He laughed. Then he cried. Then he slept. He paid too much. He left the glasses. Then he came back for them. I watched him do it.

I keep it. Now there are two things on the back seat. A tie in the glove box. Sunglasses on the leather. A man who never keeps things, keeping things.

I pick up the sunglasses. I put them in the glove box with the tie. It does not fit. I close it on the blue tie and the black glasses and the 2011 map and the unused wipes. The small space where a man could keep a thing if he wanted to.

The rain gets heavier. The wipers go.

I sit with the meter running. It says 1,240 yen. It has said 1,240 yen for nine minutes. A man can sit in a car for a long time with the meter running and go nowhere. This is allowed. This is what I do.

The son called me mother. I did not correct him. The wife knew from the third year. She never said. The woman in the back seat had my voice on a tape and did not know, and I gave it back to her and did not say.

One night. A pipe. A sign with half a character missing.

I put the car in gear.

The meter says 1,240 yen.

I drive.
