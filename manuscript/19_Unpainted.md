# CHAPTER 19 — UNPAINTED

**Yusuf · 14 February 2025 · TOKYO**

Aldi said yes. He said it in Indonesian, over a bad connection, in the middle of the road in Bekasi with a goat in the background making a noise like a small engine.

"Do it," he said. "If they look, let them look."

"They will look."

"Then let them look at you. Not at a costume."

I said nothing for a while. Forty minutes is a small country and we were in it and the goat was in it too.

"I am afraid," I said.

"Yes."

"My English is not good."

"Your English is beautiful. You say *I am in Japan for making buildings*. This is a poem."

"It is not a poem."

"It is a poem."

We laughed then. Then the signal finished on something else, as they always do, and I held the phone like a cup with no coffee, and I went to work, and I put the steel where the steel goes, and at 23:00 I went to the club with no makeup on my face, which is the most dangerous thing I have done in this country.

---

Theo put a dress on the stool in the mirror room. It was black and it had no interesting details and it was not a costume, which he said four times, in case I did not understand.

"No wig," he said. "No paint. No glitter. Nothing. You stand like you stand at the site. Hands out. That's the whole note."

"I look like a man from the site."

"Yes."

"Then why dress?"

"Because a man from the site can wear a dress." He said it as if it were a sentence from a manual, which it was, in a manual he had written in his head over six years and in three countries. "This is the whole trick, darling. The dress is not the transformation. The dress is the evidence that nothing needed transforming."

I looked in the mirror. Six mirrors held me. A man with cement on his hands and two hundred Japanese words and a letter with a window in it. The dress on the stool looked at me. I have built forty-seven floors and I have never been so frightened of a piece of cloth.

"You don't have to," Theo said.

"I know."

"No, I mean it. If you don't, I'll do the number with the broom and nobody will know the difference."

"We both know the difference."

"Yes." He touched my shoulder and took his hand away. "But I wanted you to know I know."

---

At 1:52 the bass stopped. Theo was on the scaffolding with a microphone and he said, "I am going to ask someone to do something. They said yes. So now we are both in trouble."

The room laughed. I was behind the curtain with the dress over my shirt and my hands in front of me, and I could hear my heart, which is a thing I have never heard on a building site because the machines are louder, and here there are no machines, only seventy-one people waiting.

Then I went out.

No music. This was the plan. Theo said there would be no music for the first minute, because a man walking out in a dress needs a minute in which nobody can mistake it for a number.

Seventy-one people. Pink light on wet concrete. The old man in row three with his glass moved a finger's width to the left. Rin behind the speakers with her hand on the cassette player, not pressing anything. Cass at a table with her camera on the table and her hands in her lap. Mei behind the bar, watching hands.

My hands were out.

I walked to the middle of the room, which is a short distance, four metres maybe, and it took a long time. My boots were the boots I wear on floor thirty-one. I had not cleaned them. There was cement on the left one.

Somebody coughed. Then nobody coughed. The room went quiet in a way I have never heard a room go quiet. A room with no music is a room with no wall. You can hear everyone breathing and everybody can hear you.

I stopped in the middle. I did not know what to do with my hands, so I left them out, which is what Theo said, and I looked at the room, and the room looked at me, and nobody laughed, and nobody took a photograph, and nobody shouted anything in a language I did not know.

Then Theo, from the scaffolding, very quietly, said, "There he is."

---

The music came back. Not a drag song. Not a remix. The song. The Titanic song. The one I sang in the karaoke box with Theo when the letter was in my wallet and Aldi was in Bekasi and a goat had eaten his shoe.

I started to sing it in English I do not know. This is a thing I told Aldi I would do. *If they look, let them look.*

I got the first line wrong. Then I got it right in my head. Then Theo started singing the harmony, badly, from the scaffolding, and Rin came out from behind the speakers and stood at the side of the room with her hands in her sleeves, and Mei put down the rag she was holding, and the old man in row three took his hand off his glass.

I am not a dancer. I do not know steps. I moved the way I move on a site: weight on the left foot, then the right, shoulders square, hands up, because high up you learn to keep your hands where you can see them. This is not dancing. This is scaffolding with music.

But my hands were not shaking.

My hands were up.

Halfway through the second verse I stopped. Not because the music stopped. Because I was laughing. It came out of me in a way I did not decide — a small sound, then a bigger one, standing in the middle of a basement in Shibuya in a black dress with cement on my boots and seventy-one people looking at me like I had just put the steel in the right place.

I laughed and I could not stop, and Theo stopped singing to watch me, and someone in the room started clapping, and then everybody was clapping, and the clapping was not for a good performance because there was no performance, it was for a man who had stopped being afraid for eleven seconds and had made a noise about it.

I finished the song badly. Eighty-two points out of a hundred, if the machine had been there. In my country eighty-two is fertilizer. In this country it is a room with no cameras and no form and a man in a dress singing about a ship.

---

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I have heard that sentence many times and I have never once listened to it until tonight. I stood at the side of the room in the black dress and I let it say the thing, and I did not translate it, and I did not need to, because the room had just spent four minutes being quiet with me and there was nothing to translate.

Afterwards, in the mirror room, I took off the dress and folded it and put it on the stool, and Theo came in and looked at it for a bit.

"You were good," he said.

"No."

"Something else. I don't have the word."

"In English?"

"In English." He sat on the floor against the mirror. "You know what you did. You made a room quiet. That is the only thing I have ever wanted to do and I do it by making noise."

"You made the room."

"I made a room. You made it quiet. Different jobs."

I folded the dress again, which is a thing you do with clothes, and I put it in a bag, and I said, "I will keep this."

"Good."

"And I will wear it once more."

"When?"

"When Aldi comes."

We sat there. Outside, someone shouted for ginger. The cat was somewhere near the bar, doing what the cat does.

"I am going home in March," I said.

"I know."

"Four days after the last night."

"Yes."

He did not ask me to stay, which is a new thing, and which I noticed, and which I am putting in the pocket with the other things I have decided not to pick up yet.

"Then come to the show," he said. "On the last Saturday. No cameras. Forty-eight people. Bring the dress."

"Okay."

"Bring Aldi too, if you can."

"In my head," I said. "Only in my head."

"Fine. He can be in the head. That's the best room anyway."

At three the lights came on. I went home with the dress in a bag and my boots still dirty, and on the train a woman in a suit looked at the bag and then at me and then away, and I said *otsukaresama* to her under my breath, which was wrong, and which I meant.
