# CHAPTER 16 — STRONGER NARRATIVE

**Cass · 3 February 2025 · TOKYO**

The foundation sent an email with the word *impact* in the subject line. This is a word that has done more damage to documentary ethics than any single camera manufacturer, and I say this as a person who has read the ethics guidelines of four camera manufacturers.

The email said, in essence: *we are excited about the direction of the project, and we would like to see a stronger suffering narrative.*

A stronger suffering narrative.

I read the sentence six times. On the seventh I stopped reading and started laughing, which is a thing I do when a sentence has successfully destroyed me and I want to be polite about it. My laugh in a quiet room is not attractive. It is a small dry sound, like a tape being pulled too fast.

They wanted more blood. Not more truth — more blood. They wanted the thing that fits on a festival poster with a close-up of a person who is crying in a language the audience does not need to understand because the subtitles will tell them what they already knew.

I wrote back: *I will consider this note in my shooting plan.*

This is the language of a collaborator. It is also the language of a person who has agreed to do the thing while appearing to resist it. I am very good at this. I am so good at it that I have built a career out of it and called the career *practice*.

---

I have not filmed for two months. My hands keep making the shape of the camera when I am not holding it. I have a small injury on my right thumb from gripping a thing that wasn't there. I told the doctor I had been lifting boxes. She said, *which boxes*, and I said, *camera boxes*, which was true in the way that a euphemism is true.

I have been in this room for two months with my eyes and no machine, and the results are catastrophic. I have noticed things I cannot use.

I have noticed that the old man in row three puts his glass down exactly a finger's width to the left each time he sets it down, so that by closing the glass has moved six centimetres across the table and he has never once noticed. I have noticed that Rin presses the same button on the cassette player twice, the first time to do the thing and the second time to make sure the thing has been done, and that she does this with the pill counter at the pharmacy too, I expect, though I have not filmed her there and will not. I have noticed that Yusuf says *otsukaresama* to objects. To a photograph. To a door. To the wall by the stage. This is not a mistake in his Japanese. It is a grammar he invented in which everything has worked hard and deserves to be thanked.

None of this is footage. All of it is mine in a way that footage never was, and I do not know what to do with it, because the only tool I have for keeping things is the one that turns them into evidence.

---

I asked Mei for an interview. Not a shoot. An *interview*, which is a word that sounds like a conversation and functions like a form.

We did it in 梅屋 at 4:30, before the club, with the baseball on the television and three customers, one of whom was asleep. I had the recorder on the table, not in my hand. This was a deliberate ethical choice that I have since told four people about, which tells you how ethical it actually was.

"Tell me about the club," I said.

"No."

"Tell me about why you keep it."

"I keep it because I have a mortgage."

"Mei."

She looked at the recorder. Then at me. "You want the true thing."

"I want the thing you can give."

"There is no true thing. There are things I can give and things I cannot. You want the second one because it is worth more."

I did not answer. This is the problem with Mei: she has no patience for the apparatus of my self-doubt, and my self-doubt is my favourite apparatus.

She said, "You want suffering. The foundation wants suffering. My father is sick, the building is closing, the boy has no visa, the pharmacist cannot say one word to her mother. All of this is true. You want me to put it in a sentence and hand it to you like a drink."

"I want to be honest."

"No." She wiped the counter. "You want to be *seen* being honest. Different thing."

I have thought about this sentence every day since. It is the most accurate description of my entire practice that anyone has ever given me, and it came from a woman wiping a counter while baseball played on a television in December, and she did not even raise her voice.

"Do you know why the foundation gave me money?" I said.

"Because you are good at asking."

"Because I am good at taking things from people and making them look like gifts."

"Yes."

"Do you hate me?"

"I don't have time." She put a glass down. "Hate is a luxury. Like a clean kitchen."

I laughed. She does that to me. She gives me a joke with a knife in it and I laugh because the alternative is to cry, and crying in front of a subject is a note I would never be able to edit out.

---

At 23:00 I went into the club with the recorder in my bag. Not the camera. The recorder, which is smaller and less violent and which I have been telling myself is a different instrument, which it is, which it isn't.

The room. Seventy-one people. Theo on the scaffolding with a broom, doing a joke about cleaning that is a joke about a form. Rin behind the speakers with the cassette player. Yusuf by the wall with his hands out. The old man in row three moving his glass a finger's width to the left.

Mei behind the bar, watching hands.

I stood at the edge of the room for an hour with my hands in my pockets. Then I took out the recorder and I held it for a while, and then I put it away again, and then I took it out again, which is the entire moral history of my life in three physical movements.

At 1:52 the voice came on. *You are allowed.* The room kept dancing.

I looked at the room and I tried to see it the way the foundation wants me to see it: a site of resistance, a community under threat, bodies negotiating closure. All of that is true. All of it is also a poster. I could film it right now with the recorder in my bag and it would be a good film and it would be a lie, because the thing that is actually happening is that seventy-one people are tired and happy in a room that will not exist in April, and nobody in this room wants to be a metaphor.

At 2:30 I went to the bar.

"Put it away," Mei said, without looking at me.

"I haven't taken it out."

"I can hear your breathing."

"You can't hear my breathing."

"I listen to hands. Your hands are loud tonight."

I put the recorder back in the bag. Then I said the thing I had come to say, which I had written in my head four times and which sounded worse each time:

"I am not going to film the show."

"The show is in March?"

"Yes. ORIGINS. Theo said no cameras and I said yes."

"Good."

"I want to be in it. With my eyes."

"Good."

"Do you believe me?"

Mei poured a glass and put it in front of me. She did not charge me. "I believe you today."

"Today?"

"People change by Tuesday. I only believe today."

I drank the drink. It was ginger. Everything here is ginger. 

---

At 3:12, on the street, I took out the SD card and held it in my hand under the streetlight. It is smaller than I remember. It has forty-one minutes of people who did not agree to be in my film, and one selfie I cannot delete, in which I am standing in this bar with a camera in my hand and a face that says *I am allowed to look*.

The foundation will want the suffering narrative. I will send them something. I will send them something that is true and that leaves out the parts I have decided not to pick up, which is another way of saying I will send them a lie with good lighting.

But tonight I put the card back in my pocket. I walk to the station with my hands empty, and for once they stay empty, and the sensation is so strange that I almost turn around and go back for the machine.

Almost.

That is the sentence I will not put in the grant report.
