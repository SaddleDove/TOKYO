# CHAPTER 7 — HONNE

**Rin · 19 December 2024 · Shibuya**

The rain stopped at 2:40 and started again at 2:41. Between those two minutes the street looked honest, which is to say: wet and ordinary and not trying to be a poster.

We were outside the conbini again. Not the same night as before. Last week we stood here and said small things and went home. Tonight Theo had a cut on his lip from a wig comb and he kept touching it with his finger to check whether it was still there, which it was.

He bought two rice balls. I bought one. He gave me the second one anyway, because Theo gives things away like he is throwing them, and then he is already somewhere else.

"Eat," he said.

"I ate."

"Liar."

"Not a liar. A person who ate at 11 p.m."

"Eleven is not a meal. Eleven is a snack with ambition."

I took it. The plastic was cold. He stood very close, which he does, because he is from a country where closeness is a default and distance is an insult. I am from a country where distance is a default and closeness is an apology. We are both correct and neither of us is comfortable.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what."

"The thing. The sentence you have in your mouth. You have had it since Tuesday."

I looked at the vending machine. It was lit from inside like an aquarium. There was a can of coffee with a picture of a mountain on it. I wanted to talk about the mountain.

"My mother called me sister," Theo said. "Not Theo. Not my name. *Sister.* In London she called me *son* for twenty years, and then one day she stopped, and she has never explained, and I have never asked, because asking is a door and I am afraid of what is behind it."

He said it fast, all one piece, like taking a plaster off.

"I sent her a voice note tonight," he said. "Three seconds. Breathing. I can't say *I am going to be someone you can love.* I can only breathe at her."

I bit into the rice ball. It was too cold. Everything is too cold in this country in December, including the words.

"I send money," I said. "Fifty thousand every month. Four years. She calls it insurance. She knows it is not insurance."

"So say it."

"If I say it, it becomes a thing."

"Yes."

"Then she has to answer."

"Yes."

"And if she answers wrong —"

"Then you know." He said it without drama. Theo almost never says things without drama. This is why I believed him.

I stood there with half a rice ball in my hand. The street was empty. A cleaning truck went past. It is a thing I have noticed about this city: the machines that clean are enormous and the people are small and nobody thinks this is strange.

"Do you know honne," I said.

"Is that the tape on the wall?"

"Yes."

"What does it mean, really. Not the dictionary."

I thought about it. My mother's voice saying *did you eat* for thirty years. My father saying nothing for the same period. Mei pouring a drink and looking at hands. The word on the tape, which I put up and took down and put up again.

"It means the thing under the thing," I said. "The thing you say when the room is empty."

"Tatemae is the room."

"Yes."

"And you are asking me which room we're in."

"Yes."

He looked at me then. Not at my face — at my hands. He does this sometimes. He watches the way I hold things too tightly.

"We're outside the conbini," he said. "There is no room. We are standing in the street."

"Yes."

"So maybe the true thing can be said in the street."

I have never liked this about him. He takes a word and turns it until it becomes a tool. It is very English. It is very American. It is also, occasionally, kind.

"I want to change one character," I said. "On my family register. One. To make the whole line true. To make my mother's money insurance and my father's silence something else. I called twelve times. No — eleven. I counted. They say come in person, then the form is closed, then come with a doctor's letter, then the doctor says *this will take time*, which means *this will take paperwork*. In eleven phone calls there is no sentence for *I am a woman*."

Theo did not say anything. This is a skill I did not know he had.

Then he said, "You can change your name. I can change my whole life and still not change my visa."

"Different."

"Same." He touched his lip. "You know what is funny. In London I used to shout that I was real. *Look at me, I'm here, I'm real.* Here nobody shouts. Here everyone is real in a basement for four hours and then goes home to a family register that says something else."

"A person can be real in four hours."

"A person can be real in four minutes." He put the second rice ball in my hand. "I just want more than four."

The lights inside the shop flickered. Not off. Just a small stutter, like a person deciding whether to say something.

"I want one person to know," I said. "Only one. Not my mother. Not yet. One."

"And?"

"I don't know who."

"Oh," he said. "Right. Of course."

He said it in such a small voice that I looked up. He was smiling, but it was not a joke-smile. It was the smile people use when they have been given a seat on a bus and are about to stand up again.

"I'm going to do a show," he said. "ORIGINS. In this club, before the landlord takes it. No cameras. I told Cass. She is not happy."

"Good."

"Will you help me?"

"With the show?"

"With the name. With the — I don't know. The truth part. You know the words. I only know the volume."

I put the rice ball down on the bin beside us. I had eaten enough. I am always full at exactly the wrong moment.

"Ask me again later," I said.

"Okay."

"And don't tell anyone I said honne."

"I won't."

"And don't say *I'm here* like you're throwing it."

He laughed. Then he stopped, because he had heard something in my voice, and because Theo is not stupid.

"Okay," he said. "I'll say it quietly."

At 3:03 the cleaning truck came back around the block. We both watched it go, because there was nothing else to watch, and because in this city at this hour the trucks are the only things that have somewhere to be.

"Rain," he said, in Japanese, badly.

"Rain," I said, correctly.

He grinned. "See? We're learning each other's languages."

I did not correct him. We were not. We were learning each other's silences, which is harder, and which nobody has made a textbook for.
