# CHAPTER 21 — NOTICE

**Mei · 21 February 2025 · 梅屋 / TOKYO**

Another notice came by registered post. This is a thing people in this country still do, which I respect. A fax would be more honest and less kind. A registered postman stands at your door with a clipboard and a face that says *I did not write this*, which is true, and which does not help.

I signed for it at 4:10 in 梅屋 with the baseball on the television and one customer asleep and one hiding from his wife.

There are three customers, always three. On Mondays there are two. On the first day of the month there are four, because a man comes to collect a debt that is not his and then buys a drink with the money I give him not to collect it. This is the whole economy of this bar. I am not a bartender. I am a woman with a jar of forty-seven broken openers and a very specific talent for not being asked to leave.

The notice is in a window envelope. Through the window you can see a word. The word is not *DENIED*. It is a word that means *removal* and it is printed in a font that has been chosen by a person who has never had to remove anything.

I put it in the drawer with the other notices. There are now six.

---

At 5 p.m. the landlord's son came again. He does not call. He comes in a suit and stands just inside the door and looks at the room like a man who has been sent to inspect a thing he does not want to inherit.

"My father says you should tell people," he said.

"I tell people."

"Who?"

"Nobody who matters."

"That is not —"

"Do not finish that sentence in my bar."

He finished it in his head. I could see him do it.

"Eight a.m. on the first of April," he said. "The crew has a schedule. They will start with the laundromat."

I looked up. "The laundromat?"

"The whole block. Mrs. Saito has been told."

Mrs. Saito has been told. This is worse than anything, because Mrs. Saito has worked that laundromat for twenty-two years and she has never once been told anything by anybody and she has a face that had decided nothing that opens for exactly one person, and now a man in a suit has told her that the door is coming out.

"She knows?" I said.

"Since last week."

"And she still opens?"

"She has a lease."

"She has a lease." I wiped the counter even though it was clean. "Good. Good for her."

He left. He left the envelope on the bar, but I already had one in the drawer, so there were two of the same notice in the room at the same time, which is a thing that happens in bureaucracies and in bad marriages.

財務大臣 jumped onto the stool and put one paw on the envelope. Good cat. The cat understands documents better than I do.

---

At 23:00 I open the club. Rain. The password tonight is about a ship. I do not know why. Somebody in the group chat picks these and they have a sense of humour that is either very good or very bad and I cannot tell which.

The room was full. The cat was where he always was. Theo is on the scaffolding taping something that is not a joke, which is suspicious. Yusuf is standing by the wall with his hands out of his pockets, and he has a bag with him, which I do not ask about. Rin is behind the speakers with the screwdriver again, fixing a thing that is not broken. Cass is at a table with the camera in front of her and her hands in her lap. The old man in row three moves his glass a finger's width to the left.

At 00:17 the voice comes on. *You are allowed.*

The room keeps dancing.

I stand behind the bar and watch hands and I think about Mrs. Saito, who has been told and who still opens, and who gives Theo coffee and tells him his makeup is suitable for office work, and who has never once asked me what is behind dryer four, although she must know, because she is a woman who has worked in a laundromat for twenty-two years and dryers do not open onto staircases by accident.

She knows everything. Everyone knows everything. Mrs. Saito folded a towel and did not ask about the stairs, and I wiped a counter that was already clean, and the dryer went around with somebody's underwear in it.

At 2:00 a woman comes to the bar and puts a glass down and says, "I heard about April."

"Who told you?"

"Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows nothing," I said. "Everyone knows a date. That is not the same."

She wants me to say *it will be okay*, which is a sentence that should be removed from all languages, along with *it gets better* and *think positive* and *you should forgive him*.

I gave her a drink on the house. This is not an answer. It is a drink.

---

At 3:12, after lights, after people, I take both notices out of the drawer and put them on the bar. Two identical pieces of paper. A thing said twice does not become more true, but it does become heavier.

I take out the ledger and open page 214. The last lines are:

*2024 — someone came in.*
*2024 — nobody left.*
*2025 — still here.*

Under them I write:

*April — a date.*

That's all. A date. If I write *the end* it becomes a story, and this is not a story, it is a lease with a number in it.

Then I close the book and I put it back behind the bottles nobody orders, and I put the notices back in the drawer, and I turn off the television, which is showing baseball in February because the television has never once cared about months.

I do not tell Rin. I do not tell Theo. I do not tell Yusuf or Cass or the man in row three who pays four thousand yen to leave.

They will find out from a room that is not there, which is the worst way to find out, and which is the only way, because a room is the only thing that can tell them.

Tomorrow is the twenty-second. I have thirty-eight days.

Thirty-eight is a good number. Even. Boring. A number that does not ask questions.

I put the broken openers back in the jar. Forty-seven of them. None of them work. All of them have personality.

Good night, everyone. Drink first.
