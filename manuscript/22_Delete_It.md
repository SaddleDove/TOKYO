# CHAPTER 22 — DELETE IT

**Rin · 22 February 2025 · TOKYO**

The cat stole a strip of wagyu from the bar at 1:11.

Not a whole steak. A strip. The fat was very white and the cat put his face into it like a man into a pillow, and Mei shouted something in Taiwanese, and Theo stopped mid-sentence on the scaffolding to watch, and three customers got up to help, and the cat ran behind the speakers with the meat in his mouth and looked at me.

This is 財務大臣. He is the fattest cat in Shibuya and he has never once apologised.

I laughed. I do not laugh often. It surprised me. My hand was on the cassette player at the time and I laughed into my own sleeve, and for four seconds the room was not a room with a deadline, it was a room with a cat.

Then the cat ate the wagyu and the moment was over.

---

At 1:40 I found Cass by the table at the back with the camera in front of her and her hands in her lap.

"Come outside," I said.

We went into the alley. Three bins. One broken streetlight. No music. This is where true things are said and I had decided, walking from the room to the alley, that I would say one.

"I know you filmed me," I said.

"Yes."

"On the first night. By the sink. Three minutes and eleven seconds."

She looked at me. She did not ask how I knew. This is one of her defects and one of her virtues: she expects to be found out.

"Delete it," I said.

"Rin —"

"Delete it."

"I will." She said it in the voice she uses with people who have power over her, warm, efficient, already leaving. I have heard that voice from the other side of a counter. I know exactly what it is worth.

"Do it now," I said.

"It's in a folder. There are — there are other files in the folder. It's not a simple —"

"Three minutes and eleven seconds. One file. Delete it."

"Rin, I need it."

There it is. The true thing. Not *I didn't*. Not *I forgot*. *I need it.*

I stood under the broken light. A taxi went past on the main road, far away, and I looked at it and then away, and I thought about my mother's fifty thousand yen and my father's tie and the voice in the wall that I play to a room of people who do not know it is a man.

"You need it," I said.

"I know what I'm doing is extractive. I know. I can cite —"

"Don't cite."

"I am aware of the power imbalance and I have written about it and I —"

"Don't cite at me in an alley."

She stopped. The alley has no echo. Her words just ended in the air between the bins, which is a better fate than they deserved.

Then she said, "You are the only real thing in the footage."

"I am a person."

"I know."

"No, you don't. You know an arrangement of textures. Wet hair. Grey cotton. Red knuckles. An object with a label you couldn't read." I said it flatly. I have been saving the sentence for two months and it came out without any heat, which is worse. "That's not a person. That's a shot."

She put her hand on the wall. Not dramatic. Just a hand on wet concrete because she needed somewhere to put it.

"I am going to make this film," she said. "I am going to make it whether you say yes or no, because I have a grant and a deadline and a woman I have never met who I have turned into a country. This is the truth. The other truth is that I have not filmed for two months and my hands shake when I don't, and I am frightened of a room full of people who have decided to be happy without me."

"Then be frightened in the room."

"I can't."

"Why?"

"Because I need the footage."

There it was again. The circle. A thing she says and then says again with different words, like a form that asks the same question twice in two languages.

I looked at her. I have known people who kept things past their date. Vials. Cassettes. A grey sweatshirt. A tie in a glove compartment. I am an expert in things that are kept when they should be thrown away. But there is a difference between keeping a thing and refusing to throw it away when somebody asks you to, and the difference is a person standing in an alley saying *delete it* four times.

"Because you'll only show the part that bleeds," I said.

She did not answer.

"I have a scar," I said. "You didn't film the scar. You filmed my hands. You would show my hands and tell people what they mean. You would show the vial box if I let you near it. You would put a song under it."

"That's not —"

"That's exactly what you do. You find a person and you make them into a sentence."

She took her hand off the wall. She looked at me for a long time, and her face did the thing faces do when they are being edited from the inside, when a person is deciding which parts of themselves to keep.

"I know," she said.

"Then delete it."

"I can't."

"Then you are not a witness. You are a thief."

I went back inside.

---

The room kept dancing. 財務大臣 was under the speakers with a small piece of wagyu stuck to his whisker. Theo was doing a number about a man who cannot fill in a form, which was not funny and which he did anyway. Yusuf was standing by the wall with his hands out. Mei was watching hands.

At 00:17 the voice came on. *You are allowed.*

I turned it off.

Not forever. There is a switch behind the speakers and I turned it off for eleven seconds and then I turned it back on, because the room needed it and because the room did not know what had happened in the alley and because I am not the person who decides what a room needs just because I am the one standing at the switch.

Eleven seconds. I counted.

Then I put my hand back on the player, and I made myself do the thing with the screwdriver that is not fixing, and I watched Cass come back in and sit at the table and put her hands in her lap, and the camera was still in front of her.

At 3:03 I walked home. My mother was in Kobe with my money. My father was in a taxi with my voice. A woman in Oakland had three minutes and eleven seconds of me in a folder, and she needed it, which is the worst sentence I have heard in this country, and I have heard a lot of sentences.

The rain waited until I was halfway home and then started.

I did not put my hood up. I have a jacket in a chair at home that is too big for me and smells of ginger, and I am still not giving it back, and nobody has asked me to.

This is the thing I have. Not the vials. Not the name in the box. Not the eleven phone calls. A jacket with a tear at the pocket and a man who asked instead of told.

At 3:20 I got home and put the seven pans on the counter and used the first one, which is the wrong one, and the roll was too thick and I ate it anyway.

At 3:40 I took my phone out and looked at the number for the city, which I know by heart, and I did not call it, because it is 3:40 and the form is closed, and because tomorrow is Saturday, and because I have decided not to pick something up, and today I am very good at it.

Tomorrow I will be worse.
