# CHAPTER 34 — LAST NIGHT

**Mei · 19 March 2025 · TOKYO**

Last night. The crew comes at eight in the morning. Not seven. Not nine. Eight, which is a time chosen by a man with a schedule and no imagination.

The notice said April first for eleven years. Then the son came on Monday with a new one, thinner paper, same font, and a date moved up twelve days. The twentieth. Eight a.m. He said *schedule*. I said *fine*, which is a word I have never used before in this room and which I will never use again.

I opened at eleven like always. There was no sign outside that said LAST NIGHT. I considered one. I wrote *LAST NIGHT* on a piece of paper and put it on the door and took it off again, because a sign is an admission, and an admission is a thing you make when you need someone to comfort you, and I have three customers in a failing bar and a father in a hospital and forty-seven broken openers and no intention of being comforted by a piece of paper.

Mrs. Saito came down at 10:30 with a basket of towels and a face that had decided nothing. She stood in the room for three minutes with her hands on the basket and looked at the scaffolding, the mirrors, the sink, the cat.

"Twenty-two years," she said, in Japanese.

"Twenty-two," I said.

"Where will you go?"

"Across the street. There is a bar with a licence and a fire exit and a television that shows baseball in December."

"Good." She put the basket down. "My dryer is breaking anyway."

Then she went back up. This is the whole conversation. I have written it down in my head four times and it has never become longer, which is a mercy, and which is a kind of love, and which I am not going to analyse, because analysing a thing is a way of taking it apart until you can put it in a folder.

---

Seventy-one people. One cat. Every night is seventy-one people and one cat, and tonight the seventy-one are the same seventy-one who have been here since November, which is a thing I have never seen before in a club, because clubs are made of people who come and go, and this room has become a room made of people who stayed.

The old man in row three put his glass down a finger's width to the left and then did it again and then looked at his hand, as if it had moved without him, which it had.

Yusuf was by the wall with his hands out of his pockets. He has a bag under the stool and a flight on Sunday and a dress in the bag which he will not show anyone and which he has already shown everyone by the way he stands. Cass was at a table with her camera in her bag and her hands in her lap, which is where they have been for two months. Theo was on the scaffolding in a black dress and no wig, and he has said he will do one number and he has said it four times, which means he will do three.

Rin was behind the speakers with the recorder and the tape and the screwdriver, doing the thing with the button twice, once to do the thing and once to make sure. At 00:17 she pressed play and the voice came on and the room kept dancing, and I watched her watch the speakers, and I thought about a man in a taxi with a label in blue ink in his own handwriting, and I did not say anything, because a room full of people who have decided to be happy is not a room that wants an explanation from the bartender.

At 1:40 Theo did his number. He finished on the last note, which is a thing I have never heard him do, and then he stood under the light with his hands down.

At 2:00 the old man in row three stood up and put four thousand yen on the table and walked to the door and stopped, and stood there for a while with his hand on the wall, and then he went up the stairs without looking back, and nobody clapped, because everybody knew he would be back on Tuesday, which he will not be, which is the whole joke of a room that closes, that it keeps its habits after the room is gone.

---

At 2:40 I took the ledger out from behind the bottles nobody orders. There is one page left. Not one line. One page, which is worse, because a page wants to be filled and a line does not.

I read it. Two hundred and fourteen pages of people who showed up.

*2019 — someone slept here.*
*2020 — someone didn't.*
*2022 — a broken opener. The first one.*
*2023 — a man sang in the sink. Nobody knew the words.*
*2024 — Momo let a man in who could say rain.*
*2024 — Theo's first show, the old man in row three cried, do not tell him I told you.*
*2024 — Iqbal's boots, still by the door.*
*2024 — Rin came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one.*
*2025 — Yusuf danced.*
*2024 — someone came in.*
*2024 — nobody left.*
*2025 — still here.*
*2025.02.14 — a bottle broke.*
*April — crossed out. 3.20 — a date.*
*March 15 — one show. No cameras.*

I turned to the blank page and held the pen for a while. Theo was on the stage. Yusuf was by the wall. Rin was at the switch. Cass had her hands in her lap. The cat was on the bar with one paw on my forearm, which is a thing he has started doing, which is a thing I have decided not to name, because naming a thing is a way of taking it into your hands and setting it down somewhere else.

I wrote:

*19 March — everyone came.*

Then I closed the book and put it back behind the bottles nobody orders, and for the first time in eleven years I did not take it out again at closing to check whether the line was straight.

---

At three exactly the lights came on. All of them. No warning, no countdown. Seventy-one faces get ten years older in one second, and the glue cracks on the stage like a dry field, and the wig on the stool is a small dead animal, and the cat blinks, which is the only creature in the room for whom the light changes nothing.

Nobody moved.

Not out of defiance this time. Not like Christmas. This time it was a room that had run out of places to go after three, and everybody knew it, and nobody needed to make a decision about it, because the decision had been made in November by a landlord with a wedding ring on the wrong hand.

Theo sat down on the edge of the stage. Rin came out from behind the speakers and stood beside him. Yusuf came away from the wall. Cass took her hands out of her lap and put them on the table and then on her knees. The old man was gone, but his glass was still there, four thousand yen under it, which Theo will find in the morning and which nobody will know what to do with, and which I will put in the new bar behind the register under a piece of paper that says *if*.

At 3:12 people started to go. Slowly. In ones and twos. Not because the music stopped — I left the bass on, very low, the way I did on Christmas — but because a room can only hold a thing for so long before the thing becomes too heavy to stand under, and a room with no tomorrow is the heaviest room there is.

By 3:40 there were four of us and a cat.

Theo took off the dress and folded it and put it in a bag, and he took the sunglasses out of his pocket and put them on the stool beside the wig, and then he took them back, and then he put them in his pocket again, which is a thing I have seen him do with that object six times tonight and which I have decided is a private matter.

Rin said something to him in a voice too low for the room, which is a thing people do in a room with no music, and he nodded, and she put two fingers on the edge of the stage and left.

Yusuf said *otsukaresama* to the door on the way out, which is wrong, which he knows is wrong, which he says anyway.

Cass put the camera in her bag and zipped it, which is a sound I have heard twice in four months and which I am putting in the ledger in my head, because there is no room on the page.

---

At 4 a.m. I turned off the lights. Not at three. Later. For the second time in my life I broke my own rule, and I broke it alone, and nobody clapped, and the cat did not care.

I washed the glasses. All of them. One by one. The Claudia glass, the gin glass, the old man's glass with four thousand yen under it, which I washed and dried and put back on the shelf in row three, because somebody has to keep the row.

財務大臣 sat on the stool and watched me. Good cat. The best cat. I have decided not to say why.

At 4:40 I put the ledger in my bag. Not behind the bottles. In my bag. I have never done this before. I have never taken that book out of this building, and the building will be a wall in four hours, so there is nowhere left to put it, which is the whole logic of a thing that ends.

On the way up the stairs I stopped at the door with no handle and put my hand on it, the way Momo used to stand there with his hand out, and I did not say the password, because there was nobody to say it to, and because the password tonight was a sentence about a door, which somebody picked, and which I suspect Rin picked, and which she will deny, with too much detail, if I ask.

WASH & GO was bright and empty and the dryers were going. Mrs. Saito was behind the counter folding towels. She did not look up. She said, in English, "Your makeup today is very suitable for office."

"Thank you," I said.

"Tomorrow is not office."

"No."

"Then it is fine."

Outside, the cleaning truck was brushing the gutters. It was 4:58. In three hours and two minutes a crew of men in hard hats would come and take the room apart, starting with the laundromat, and nobody would put it back, and nobody would believe it had been there, which is why I am carrying a book with two hundred and fourteen pages of people who showed up.

A room with a door that opens. That is the whole product.

I sold it for four hours at a time for four months, and then I gave it away, and I would do it again, which is the stupidest thing I have ever done, and which is the only thing I have ever done properly.

The rain waited until I was at the station and then started, the way it does.

It had been waiting all night.
