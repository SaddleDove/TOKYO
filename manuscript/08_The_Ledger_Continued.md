# CHAPTER 8 — THE LEDGER, CONTINUED

**Mei · 22 December 2024 · 梅屋 / TOKYO**

I have forty-seven bottle openers. All of them are broken. People come in and see the jar behind the bar and they say, *why do you keep broken openers*, and I say, *they have personality*, and then they laugh because they think it's a joke. It is not a joke. A broken opener is an honest object. It tells you exactly what it can't do.

People think bartenders listen to stories. We don't. We listen to hands. Hands don't lie.

Theo's hands: shake before, still during, shake after. Rin's hands: never. Yusuf's hands: cement, always, even when he washes, because cement is not dirt, it is a decision the skin has made. Cass's hands: everywhere, always, holding a machine, like a person who is afraid that if she puts it down she will have to be in the room.

My hands: fine. My hands are fine. This is the least interesting thing about me.

---

The money comes from a company that does not exist. This is not a crime exactly. It is a thing a lawyer helped me set up in 2015, and the lawyer is now dead of a thing that happens to lawyers, and the company sends money to TOKYO every month on the 28th, and nobody knows it is me.

Three hundred thousand yen. Sometimes more. On the anniversary of the accident, six hundred thousand. This is the money from the settlement, which is a word that means: *we will pay you so that you never say the word again.*

I put it into an account with a name like a small grey animal and the money comes out on the 28th and goes into the club's account, and Mei Ling is not connected to either side of the transaction. Clean. Boring. Excellent.

I do not tell anyone because if I tell anyone it becomes a favour, and a favour is a bill with feelings.

Theo found out because Theo finds out things. He is very good at finding out things and very bad at keeping them. He came in at 5 p.m. to 梅屋, which is a failing bar that opens at three and closes at six and has three customers, one of whom is here to hide from his wife. The television is always on and it is always baseball. In December it should not be baseball, but the television does not care about months.

He sat at the bar and said, "Your accountant is a woman called Chika in Shinjuku."

"Good morning to you too."

"She handles the club's account. She handles yours. Same hands."

"So?"

"So the anonymous donor is — "

"Don't."

He stopped. He is quite good at stopping when a person says don't, which is one of the reasons I like him, although I would never tell him, and if I did tell him I would deny it in a court of law.

"You don't want thanks," he said.

"I don't want a scene."

"Same thing."

"No." I put a glass down. "Thanks is a bill. A scene is a fire. I don't want either."

He looked at me for a while. Then he said, in that voice of his that arrives from about three countries away, "When my father cut me off, I had four hundred pounds and a suitcase. You know what I did? I told everyone. I told the whole of East London. *Look what he did. Look what he did.* I thought if enough people knew, it would stop being true."

"Did it?"

"No." He took a drink. "It made it heavier. But at least everyone could see the shape of it."

I wiped the counter. I have wiped this counter for eleven years and it has never once become clean.

"You want a story?" I said. "I got stories. They're all the same story. That's the problem."

---

At 6:20 the landlord came himself. Not the son. The landlord.

He is seventy-four and he wears a wedding ring on the wrong hand and he used to drink at 梅屋 in 2011 and he used to call me *mei-chan*, which is a thing you can call a person when you are paying for their father's mistakes and not otherwise.

He stood at the door with the envelopes in his hand. Two of them. One for 梅屋, one for the room behind the dryer. He did not sit down.

"April first," he said.

"You said ninety days."

"Ninety days from the first notice." He put the envelopes on the bar. "This is another notice. This is different."

"It is always different."

"Yes," he said. " He did not laugh. This time it is different."

I almost laughed. I did not. He has a face that has been forgiven too many times by people who needed him to be a decent man, and it shows.

He looked around the bar. At the baseball on the television. At the jar of broken openers. At my hands on the counter.

"You know what your father did to me," he said. Not a question.

"He lost money."

"He lost my money."

"He lost money."

We looked at each other. This is a thing that happens in Taiwanese families: everyone knows the shape of the debt and nobody says the number, because the number is a person, and the person is your father.

He put his hat back on. "I am sorry about your father," he said. "His head is bad now."

"His head was bad before."

"Yes." He went to the door. Then he stopped. "You should tell people about the room behind the dryer. When it is gone, nobody will believe it was there."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because a thing nobody believes is a thing that never happened."

Then he left. I watched him go down the street, and 財務大臣 came in and jumped onto the stool and put one paw on the second envelope, and I let him, because he is right about everything.

---

At 23:00 I open TOKYO. Rain. Seventy people. Theo is behind the curtain already, taping something to the scaffolding, whispering to Rin about a word he wants to use in the show. Cass is in the corner with the camera on her knee, not filming. Yusuf is standing near the sink with his hands in his pockets like a man who has been told he can stay and does not believe it.

At 00:17 the voice comes on. *You are allowed.* The room keeps dancing.

I take the ledger out and open page 214. There is one blank line. I take the pen out and I hold it for a while.

Then I write:

*2024 — the bill was paid by the person who owed it.*

Not *a visitor*. Not *a boy from London*. Someone. A person is the smallest word that is still big enough.

I close the book. I put it back behind the bottles nobody orders. I do not show it to anybody. A ledger is not a gift. It is a bill with someone's name on it, and names are complicated in this country, and they are complicated in mine too.

Three minutes. Theo is still on stage. At three exactly we both stop pretending.

Broken opener, broken bar, broken father, broken city. It is a very good room.
