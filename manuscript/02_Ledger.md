# CHAPTER 2 — LEDGER

**Mei · 28 November 2024 · 梅屋 / TOKYO**

Forty-eight people. Fire code says forty-eight. Tonight there are seventy-one and one cat, and the cat is not on the permit.

I know this because I counted. I count everything. Cups, ice, the number of times a man says *just one more* and then has three more. Money, of course. Money is the easiest thing in the world to count and the hardest thing in the world to keep.

I do not ask stories. Stories are expensive and nobody pays. I watch hands. Hands tell me everything. Theo's hands shake before a show and stop the second the mic feeds back. Rin's hands never shake. This is the problem with Rin. Somewhere in her body there is a shaking that never got permission to come out.

The new one came in at 23:20. Wet hair, borrowed rain, sunglasses on his face like he was hiding from a country that had not yet noticed him. He ordered the ginger thing before he knew what the ginger thing was. This is either confidence or hunger. With men it is usually hunger.

"Name," I said.

"Theo."

"No." I slid the glass. "Not here."

"That's my name."

"It's a lovely name." I poured. "Drink first."

He drank it like he wanted the glass to explain something to him. Then he looked at the speakers, because everyone looks at the speakers sooner or later, and he said, "Who is that?"

"Tokyo," I said.

He believed me. Of course he believed me. He had been here eleven hours and he was already in love with a room.

In eleven hours, a person can fall in love with a room. In sixteen years, a person can fall out of love with a country and still have to live in it. This is the difference between a visitor and a resident. A visitor gets to arrive.

---

The ledger is under the bar, behind the bottles nobody orders. It is a Chinese accounting book with a red cover and 214 pages and it does not contain money. Money is in the bank, where it belongs, being boring.

The ledger contains this:

*2019 — someone slept here.*
*2020 — someone didn't.*
*2022 — a broken opener. The first one.*
*2023 — a man sang in the sink. Nobody knew the words.*
*2024 — Momo let a man in who could say rain.*
*2024 — Rin came in with wet hair and I thought, there's one.*

That's the whole book. Two hundred and fourteen pages of that. People think a ledger is about debt. No. A ledger is about who showed up.

Tonight I have not written in it yet. I will, after closing. There is a line at the bottom of page 214 that is still blank, and I am saving it, which is stupid, because a blank line is not a saving. It's just a blank line.

---

My father called me sister today.

Yesterday he called me by my name. Tomorrow maybe nothing. I shave his head every Sunday. He holds still for me like he used to hold still for nothing. When he was strong he held still for nobody. He was a man who walked through a room like he owned the floor. Then the floor took him back.

I got the call at 4:20, before opening. My sister — no, not my sister, my cousin, who lives in Tokyo and has a face like an unpaid bill — said he had been sitting in the bath for two hours and would not come out because there was a woman in the house.

"Tell him I'm not a woman," I said.

"Oh, good," she said. "That will be very helpful."

I went over at five. He was in the bath, fully dressed, which is a thing he has started doing. His cardigan was floating. He looked up at me with those eyes that are still his eyes and said, in Taiwanese, "妹妹, have you eaten?"

I said, "I ate."

I did not eat. I shaved his head in the bathroom with the cardigan still on because he would not let me take it off, and he held still, and afterwards he said thank you in Japanese, which he never used to say, because Japanese was for customers.

He called me sister again on the way out. I know he means *daughter* in the way a mind that is leaving a body sometimes drops the right word on the floor and keeps walking. I know this. It does not make the word smaller.

---

Three minutes to closing. Theo is still on stage. Somebody is crying in the mirror room — somebody always is — and I put the ginger in and I don't look at the clock. The clock and I, we have an understanding: it does its job, I do mine, and at three exactly we both stop pretending.

財務大臣 is eating the garnish again.


---

At 23:41 the landlord's son came by. Not the landlord. The landlord has not come by in four years because the landlord is in Taiwan and because the landlord is my father's old friend, which is a nicer word for creditor than I would choose. The son is thirty and wears a suit that costs more than my fridge. He stood at the door of 梅屋 with an envelope in his hand and the face of a man who has been told to do something and is enjoying it more than he should.

"Ninety days," he said. "My father says ninety days."

"Your father says many things," I said. "Most of them to a lawyer."

"This is different."

"It is always different."

He put the envelope on the bar. I did not pick it up. 財務大臣 jumped onto the stool beside it and put one paw on the paper, and for one beautiful second the cat was a better lawyer than either of us.

"April first," the son said. "The whole block. They already have the permits."

"April first," I said. "Of course. Funny month for it."

He did not laugh. He was not there for laughs.

When he left, I opened the envelope. It was a notice. It was in Japanese and it was very clean and it said the word for demolition as if it were the word for lunch.

I put it in the drawer with the other notices. There are four. I have never shown any of them to anyone, not even to Rin, who would read it three times and then ask a question I could not answer in a way that would satisfy either of us.

Ninety days is a long time if you are counting forward from a door closing. It is a very short time if you are counting backward from a room that has to be emptied.

---

People ask me why I keep this place. I give them the answer with money in it because that is the answer they can hold. Rent. Debt. The loan on the bar. True, all true, none of it the reason.

The reason: in 2009 I was locked in a room for three days in Changhua and on the fourth day my aunt opened the door and said nothing, and that nothing was the only kindness I got that year. A room with a door that opens. That is the whole product. I sell doors that open.

You want a story? I got stories. They're all the same story. That's the problem.

---

02:58. Theo is still on stage, which is fine. He is still on stage at 02:58 every night because he believes the lights are a suggestion. They are not a suggestion. They are a landlord with better lighting.

The new one — Rin — is behind the speakers. She does not dance. She does not drink. She came in with wet hair and stood near the utility sink for forty minutes doing something with a small recorder and a cassette, and when I asked her what she was doing she said, "Work," in the voice of a person who has decided that the word is a locked door.

Fine. I do not ask stories.

At three exactly, the lights come on. All of them. No warning, no countdown. Seventy-one faces get ten years older in one second. Theo's glue cracks like a dry field. The queen in the corner stops mid-gesture, which is a kindness, because nobody should have to finish a gesture under fluorescent light.

People go. They always go. They go slowly, because leaving a room you love is a physical act, like pulling a plaster off a wound. The new one stays by the sink. Theo comes down from the stage and stands in the light looking like a man who has been evicted from a country, which he has not yet been.

"Four hours," he says to nobody.

"Four hours," I say, because it is true and because he needs someone to say it back.

I take the ledger out from behind the bottles. I open to page 214. The blank line is waiting. I write on it:

*someone came in.*

Then I put the ledger back. 財務大臣 sits on it.

Fine. The cat can have the last word.
