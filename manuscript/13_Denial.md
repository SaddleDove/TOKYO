# CHAPTER 13 — DENIAL

**Yusuf · 10 January 2025 · Shibuya → Bekasi (screen)**

The letter comes in an envelope with a window in it. Through the window you can see a word. The word is *DENIED*. It is in English because Aldi sent the form in English, because my English is two hundred words and his is four hundred and he is braver than me.

I hold the envelope for nine minutes. I do not open it. A closed envelope is a thing with a future. An open envelope is a thing with a fact.

Then I open it. The fact is there. It has a date and a stamp and a box that is ticked, and the tick is very small, and it has more authority than my body, which is thirty-one floors tall and made of cement.

Aldi cannot come.

He cannot come because he is a man who loves a man and this country has not written that sentence into the form. The form is beige. This is the detail I cannot get over. A thing that decides your life is beige and has a box.

I put the letter in my wallet, in the place where my mother's photograph is, and when Aldi calls at 22:00 I say nothing about it for twenty-one minutes.

---

We talk about the rain. It is raining in Bekasi. It is raining in Shibuya. We talk about the rain because the other things need more than forty minutes.

"Your mother goat," I say. "Is okay?"

"She is fine. She ate my shoe."

"Which shoe?"

"Both."

We laugh. Forty minutes is a small country and we live in it and we have a goat.

"Your work," he says. "How is your work."

"Steel."

"Always steel."

"Always steel."

Then he says something in Indonesian that I cannot translate into English, and I know what it means anyway, and I do not answer, because my Indonesian is perfect and my English is broken and neither of them has a sentence for this.

At 22:34 he says, "Yus, I have news."

I take the letter out. I put it on the table. I hold the phone so the screen can see it, which is stupid, because the screen is in Bekasi and the paper is in Shibuya and there is no way for one to touch the other.

"I have news," I say.

"You first."

"You."

"No, you."

"Aldi—"

"Yusuf, just say."

The signal finishes. This is the thing about forty minutes. It does not care about what you were saying. It ends on the word, and the word stays in your mouth, and you are holding a phone like a cup with no coffee.

I call back. No answer. I send a message. It says *I am fine*, which is a lie in two languages.

---

Thursday. Karaoke. Theo takes me because Theo takes people places the way other people take umbrellas, automatically and with poor judgement.

It is a small room with a machine that has a broken microphone and a screen with the words in three languages. Iqbal would have loved it. Iqbal would have sung every song and then sung them again.

Theo sings something by a woman with a very large voice. He sings it as a joke for the first thirty seconds and then he forgets it's a joke, and he is suddenly a man standing in a karaoke box in Shibuya with his whole face in a song he has no right to sing, and I look away, because a person should be allowed to be serious in private.

Then he hands me the microphone.

"I am going to sing the Titanic song," I say.

"Of course you are."

"You know it?"

"Every drag queen in the world knows the Titanic song. It's in the contract."

I sing it. In English. Badly. I get the words wrong and then I get them right in my head and then I get them wrong again, and Theo is on the floor laughing, not at me, with me, and when I get to the part about the heart going on I stop, because my heart is not going on, it is sitting in an envelope with a window in it, and a small tick has decided where it can live.

Theo stops laughing. He does not say *are you okay*. He says, "Again. And this time I'm singing the harmony, which I do not know."

So we do it again. Two men in a karaoke box in Shibuya at 1 a.m., one from London and one from Java, singing a song about a ship going down, in an accent between three countries and a language neither of us was born in.

The machine gives us 82 points. A score out of 100 for a thing that cannot be scored.

"That's —" I stop. "That is very good," I say.

"Eighty-two is a pass," Theo says. "In my country eighty-two is a university."

"In my country eighty-two is fertilizer."

"Then we are very educated farmers."

We laugh again. This is how a man survives a beige form. Not with dignity. With a bad song and a friend who knows the harmony he does not know.

---

At 23:00 I go to TOKYO. I put the letter in my wallet. The password tonight is about a window. I say it wrong. Momo holds out his hand, and I pass.

Inside, the room. Theo is doing a number with a broom, which is a joke about cleaning, which is a joke about housework. The room laughs. Rin is behind the speakers. Cass is in the corner with her camera on the table. Mei looks at my hands and says nothing, which is how I know she has seen the envelope.

At 00:17 the voice comes on. *You are allowed.*

I stand by the wall. Two hundred words. A beige form. A goat in Bekasi. A man in row three with a grey coat who is not my father and who has never denied anything.

The voice says the sentence and the room keeps dancing, and for four hours I am a man who can be in a room without a form.

At 3:03 I call Aldi. He answers on the second ring.

"I have news," I say.

"Tell me."

"The letter—"

"Yus."

"The letter is — it is not good."

There is a silence. Not a bad silence. A silence with a goat in it somewhere.

"I know," he says.

"You know?"

"I saw the photo. You sent the photo before you told me. You are bad at secrets."

"I am bad at everything."

"No. You are bad at secrets. Different."

Forty minutes. We have maybe six left and we spend them on nothing. On the goat. On the rain. On the shape of a blue tarp over a motorbike.

Then: "Aldi, I—"

The signal finishes.

This time I do not call back. I sit on the step outside WASH & GO with the phone in my hand, and I say *otsukaresama* to the screen, which is wrong, which is always wrong, and I go inside, because Mrs. Saito is waving at me through the glass and she has a towel in her hand and a face that had decided nothing that has decided, tonight, to open a little.

"Coffee?" she says.

"Yes," I say. "Please."

I have two hundred Japanese words. *Please* is one of them. It is the most useful one.
