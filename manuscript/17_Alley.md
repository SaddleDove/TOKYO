# CHAPTER 17 — ALLEY

**Rin · 9 February 2025 · TOKYO, back alley**

The alley has three bins, one broken streetlight, and a cat called 財務大臣 who owns all of it. There is no music here. This is why all the true things are said here and not in the room, where the bass makes everything sound like a decision.

It is 1:40 and Theo is smoking, which he has started again since January, and I do not smoke, but I stand next to him because the alley is narrow and there is nowhere else to stand that is not a bin.

"Your mother," he said. "Just say it."

"She knows."

"She knows and she is waiting. That's different."

"Different how."

"If she knows, she is waiting for you. If you tell her, she has to do something with it."

"She has been doing something with it for four years. Fifty thousand a month. She calls it insurance."

"Then call it something else."

I looked at the bins. One of them had a sticker with a word I could not read. The light was broken in the way lights are broken here: not off, just worse.

"It is not a word problem," I said.

"Everything is a word problem with you."

"No." I turned to him. "Everything is a form problem. A number problem. A doctor who says *this will take time* when he means *this will take paperwork*. I go to the city and they say come in person and the form is closed and come back Tuesday with a letter, and on Tuesday the woman at the window says the letter is the wrong letter, and I go home and take the pills out of the box and put them back in the box, and my mother sends money for insurance, and my father drives a taxi past this alley every night and does not stop."

He stopped smoking. He was listening, which he does with his whole body, which is annoying, because then you cannot take anything back.

"I saw the taxi," I said. "On Christmas. I saw it parked."

"So?"

"So nothing. He didn't get out."

"Do you want him to?"

"No."

"Do you want him to know?"

"No."

"Then why did you tell me?"

I did not answer. A cat came out from behind a bin and looked at us and went back in. 財務大臣 does not care about true things. This is why I like him.

Theo put the cigarette out on the wall. Then he said, in the voice he uses when he is trying very hard not to make a joke, "I told my mother I was a woman. In a voice note. Three seconds. I breathed at her. That was the whole message."

"She wrote back."

"Two words."

"Good."

"Yes. Good." He took off his glasses and put them in his pocket. Without them his face looked unfinished. "Rin. In London I used to say it all the time. *I'm here. This is me. Look.* I thought saying it was the same as being it. Then I came here and I met you and you have a name in a box and you take it out and put it back in and it is still yours, and you have never once shouted it, and you are more —" he stopped. "You are more *it* than I have ever been."

"I am not brave."

"You are not loud. Different thing."

He stepped closer. Not close in the way men step close when they want something. Close in the way people step close when the alley is cold. He took off his jacket — a black jacket with a tear at the pocket, the one he wears over the wig cases — and put it over my shoulders, which is a stupid thing to do because I am wearing a sweatshirt and he is wearing a shirt and it is February.

"Put it on," he said.

"I'm fine."

"Put it on, you stupid pharmacist."

I put it on. It smelled of smoke and cheap laundry detergent and ginger. It was too big, like the sweatshirt, like every piece of clothing I own, like the word *woman* when I take it out of the box and hold it up.

"Don't tell me what to do," I said.

It came out harder than I meant it. He looked at me and for a second there was no joke in his face at all, and I could see that there never had been, underneath, which is a horrible thing to see in a person who has been funny for twenty-four years.

"Rin—"

"Don't tell me what to do."

He put his hands in his pockets and stepped back, and the jacket stayed on my shoulders, and the cat came out again and sat on a bin and watched us with the face of a small grey accountant.

Behind the wall the bass came back on. Somewhere inside, someone shouted for ginger.

"I wasn't telling you," he said.

But the music was loud and I did not hear him, or I heard him and decided not to, which is a different thing and which I have decided not to pick up.

At 2:58 the lights came on through the wall, not in the alley — the alley has its own broken light, which is worse — but you could hear the room change, the way a room changes when a door opens and somebody leaves it open.

I did not give the jacket back.

---

At 3:07 I walked to the conbini alone. The rice balls were in the same place. The lights were the same bright hospital lights. I bought one and stood outside with Theo's jacket on and my hands in the sleeves, which is a thing I have never done with a jacket in my life.

A taxi went past slowly on the main road. Not his. Yellow lights, no passenger, the number on the roof lit up. I watched it go and then I watched the place where it had been, which is a stupid thing to do and which I do every time.

My mother is in Kobe. She has my fifty thousand and she has a word for it that is not true, and she waits for me to say a sentence that I have in my mouth and cannot get out. My father is in a taxi and he knows a voice that I play in a room and he has never once come through the dryer. The two of them are in the same city and they have not spoken in nine years and I am the reason and I am not the reason.

Theo says *say it*. Theo says *look at me*. Theo throws sentences like he is throwing things from a moving train, and they land, and he is already somewhere else.

But he gave me the jacket.

Here is the thing about the jacket: it is not a solution. It is not a sentence. It is a piece of clothing with a tear at the pocket, and it is warm, and it is his, and he did not ask me to wear it correctly.

I ate the rice ball. It was cold. Everything is cold in February.

At 3:20 I went home. I put the jacket over the back of the chair and I put the vial box back in the fridge and I took out the seven pans and used the second one, which is the correct one, and I made an omelette that was too sweet, and I ate it standing up.

Outside, the rain started again. It had been waiting all night. It is patient, this rain. It has been falling since March and it will still be falling in November, and nobody has ever once told it to stop, and nobody ever will.

I put the jacket on again before I went to bed. I did not tell anyone. This is not a secret. It is just a thing I have decided not to pick up and examine.
