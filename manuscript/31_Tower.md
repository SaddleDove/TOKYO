# CHAPTER 31 — TOWER

**Rin · 13 March 2025 · TOKYO / a parked taxi**

Mei let me in at 1 p.m. because I said the wire to the left speaker was loose and because I have learned that a technical sentence opens more doors than a true one.

The room in daylight is a different animal. Wet concrete, grey plastic chairs stacked on tables, the smell of ginger and old glue and mop water. The mirror room with its six mirrors and its strips of paper. The sink with three metres of stainless steel and no cups on it. The stage with the scaffolding, which is called 東京タワー, Tokyo Tower, which is two metres high and which nobody in this room has ever gone up, because the room has decided it is not for anybody.

Mei said, "Don't fall."

"I won't."

"I am not asking."

"I know."

She went back behind the bar to count something, and I put the screwdriver in my pocket and climbed.

---

The scaffolding is not a tower. It is four pipes and a board with duct tape on the edges. But it is two metres up and from up there the room looks like what it is: a rectangle with a leak.

There is a name carved into the board.

Not painted. Carved. A small knife or a key or a piece of metal, one letter at a time, in English, badly, like a man writing a sentence he has waited twenty-five years to be allowed to write.

KENJI.

Under it, smaller: 1999.03.

And under that, a word in Japanese which is not a name and which I know because I have said it to myself in the mirror for four years. 東京. TOKYO.

I put my finger in the groove of the K. The wood is old and the groove is deep and somebody pressed hard enough to split the grain, which is a thing a person does when they are not sure the name will stay.

I stood up there for six minutes. The room did not change. The room never changes. This is the whole problem with rooms: they hold things and they do not tell you.

I have been playing a voice in this room for four months. A man's voice. Bad English. Careful. A small dry laugh. A sentence under the breath in Japanese that I have played fifty times and still could not decide whether it was a prayer or an address.

I got down. I did not fall. Mei did not come out from behind the bar.

---

At 1:40 a.m. a taxi was parked on Maruyamacho. Far from the laundromat. Far enough that the sign was small.

I walked to it and opened the back door and got in.

"Shibuya," I said.

"We are in Shibuya."

"Then drive."

He did not drive. He put the hand brake on and he sat with both hands on the wheel, looking through the windscreen at the rain.

I had the cassette in my bag. I had the recorder. I had the screwdriver, which I had forgotten to take out of my pocket and which was not for him and which I put on the seat between us so that he would not think it was a weapon, which is a thing I decided in the second before I put it down.

"The voice," I said.

He said nothing.

"Why did you let me use it."

He watched the meter. It said 1,240 yen. It had said 1,240 yen for nine minutes, which I knew because I had been standing outside the car for six minutes and sitting in it for three, and because counting is what I do.

"It was already in the room," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he said. "It's the answer."

The wipers went. The rain was the same rain as November and as March and as every night for four months.

"You knew it was your voice," I said. "In the car. On the twelfth. You picked up the tape and you saw your writing and you gave it back to me and you said nothing."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"No shape for it."

"There is a shape. There is a word for it."

"Not in my English."

"In Japanese there is one."

"I don't use that word."

"Which word?"

He put the heat on. Then he turned it off. A man who does not sleep does not need heat.

"You were nine," he said.

I looked out of the window. The laundromat was bright from inside and Mrs. Saito was folding towels and a dryer was going and nobody in there knew that a man was sitting in a cab outside with a tie in the glove compartment and a voice in a box on the back seat.

"You were nine," he said again. Not louder. The same. He has one sentence and he says it twice, which is his whole vocabulary of apology.

"I was nine," I said.

"You believed it."

"I believed it."

"I know."

That is the sentence that broke it. Not *I'm sorry*. Not *I am your father*. Not *I wanted to be*. Just: *I know*. A man who heard a tape at nine under a kotatsu and a man who stood on a pipe at twenty-six and said the same sentence into a microphone in a room under a pachinko hall, and the tape went into a drawer and a flea market and a cheap stereo, and the two of them sat in a taxi twenty-five years later with a meter that had stopped at 1,240 yen.

I did not forgive him.

He did not ask.

---

"I need to know one thing," I said.

"Ask."

"Did you tell her. My mother. Did you tell my mother about the tape."

"No."

"She knew."

"From the third year."

"Did you ever say it."

"No."

"Why not."

"I was good at being her husband."

"Were you happy?"

"Yes." A pause. "No. Both. I was good at it."

I put my hand on the screwdriver and took it off again. I have a great many objects in my hands in this conversation and none of them help.

"I changed one character," I said. "On the family register. Eleven phone calls. A doctor who said *this will take time*. In November. I have the letter in my room."

He looked at me in the mirror. Not at my face. At the back of my head, which is how he sees me now, which is how he has always seen me, which is the whole problem.

"Are you a woman," he said.

"Yes."

"Then it is done."

"It is not done. It is eleven phone calls and a letter and a doctor's sentence. It is not the same as being one."

"Same where I live."

"Where you live is a taxi."

"Yes."

I laughed. I did not want to. It came out the way the laugh came out in the alley that night with the cat and the wagyu, which is to say: against my whole architecture.

He did not laugh. But his hand moved on the wheel. A small movement. Two centimetres. The same movement from the first night, when the voice came through the wall and he was driving a drunk foreign man who said Shibuya when we were in Shibuya.

I opened the door.

"Rin," he said.

I stopped with one foot on the pavement.

"I have something," he said.

He opened the glove compartment. It did not fit. He closed it on the tie and the sunglasses and the 2011 evacuation map and the unused wet wipes, and then he opened it again and took out the blue tie, which had been folded badly for three weeks, and he held it out.

"For Yuma," I said.

"For you."

"I am not Yuma."

"I know."

I stood in the rain with the door open and the tie in his hand between us, which is a thing a father gives a daughter in this country and in mine and in none of the languages I speak properly.

I took it. It was still warm from the car.

"Wear it," I said.

"I never wore it."

"Then don't start."

I got out. I shut the door. I walked to the laundromat through the rain with my hands in my sweatshirt sleeves and the cassette in my bag and a screwdriver in my pocket that I had not used on any wire.

At 2:01 I pressed play. At 2:41 I pressed play again. The room kept dancing. The voice said the sentence to seventy-one people who did not know that a man was sitting in a parked car outside with the meter at 1,240 yen, and I stood at the switch with my hand on it, and for the first time in four months I let the whole thing run without counting the seconds.

Not forgiveness. Not reconciliation. A room with a voice in it and a woman who finally knew whose.

At three the lights came on. I went outside. The taxi was gone.

In the morning, on the board under the stage, I put a piece of masking tape over the name. Not to hide it. To mark it. On the tape I wrote one word in English, because it is the only language the room understands:

*STAY.*

Then I went to work and counted three hundred and forty-two pills and did not count the ones I take, which is a different kind of counting, and which I have decided to stop lying about on Tuesdays.
